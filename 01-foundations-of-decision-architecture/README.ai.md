Decision Architecture is presented through six foundations for understanding decisions. They are not a definition, process, checklist, or prescriptive method, but recurring characteristics that explain what is involved when a decision is considered, made, and produces effects. Their purpose is to provide a conceptual layer before selecting techniques or frameworks: understand the decision and the needs created by its characteristics, then identify suitable practices. Every decision occurs within one or more domains and a particular context, pursues objectives, involves uncertainty, produces consequences, and changes the conditions for subsequent decisions. These dimensions interact continuously rather than forming sequential stages.

Every decision occurs within one or more domains of knowledge, activity, or problem. The domain determines the concepts, knowledge, criteria, and practices used to interpret the situation and evaluate alternatives. Complex decisions can cross domains such as architecture, security, finance, product, operations, and regulation, with each perspective exposing different concerns and applying different criteria. Specialized knowledge informs a decision but does not determine its outcome, since decisions within the same domain can differ according to their objectives, context, constraints, and alternatives. Practices developed for one domain should therefore not be transferred automatically to another. The resulting need is domain delimitation: identify the relevant fields, knowledge, participants, and boundaries, including intentional intersections between domains. Scope definition, boundary identification, domain modeling, stakeholder identification, and common language address this need.

A decision starts from an existing situation whose relevant conditions form its context. Domain identifies the field of the decision; context identifies the particular circumstances in which it occurs, including resources, constraints, information, assumptions, commitments, dependencies, and previous decisions. Different contexts within the same domain can produce different viable alternatives and evaluation criteria. Context also changes over time as information appears, resources are consumed, constraints shift, and previous decisions produce effects. Experience provides patterns and references, but their applicability depends on the current situation. The resulting need is to make the decision conditions explicit through diagnosis, constraint and assumption identification, dependency analysis, and understanding of the current state. This is also necessary when applying frameworks: a method can be executed correctly while being inappropriate for the actual conditions.

Objectives represent the outcomes a decision intends to achieve, preserve, or avoid. They may be explicit or implicit and depend on the domain and context in which they are defined. Making them explicit establishes what matters when alternatives are compared and exposes conflicts such as cost versus quality, speed versus risk, or flexibility versus complexity. Objectives may change as analysis reveals new information or exposes infeasibility or previously overlooked outcomes. Since a situation becomes a problem in relation to an intended outcome, changing the objective can also change the definition of the problem. The resulting need is evaluation criteria: requirements, metrics, indicators, quality attributes, success conditions, or acceptable limits that make the relationship between alternatives and objectives assessable. An objective does not necessarily need a numerical metric; it needs a sufficiently clear basis for evaluation.

Decision-making necessarily involves uncertainty because relevant information about the situation, alternatives, consequences, or future conditions is incomplete. Uncertainty can concern the problem itself, whether a solution will work, user behavior, external dependencies, or future changes. Its treatment depends on its nature: investigation can improve understanding, experiments can test solutions, and risk or scenario analysis can examine uncertain consequences. Information gathering, expert consultation, hypotheses, prototypes, and experiments all have costs, while waiting can both improve knowledge and create opportunity costs. The relevant question is therefore how much uncertainty should be reduced before acting, considering the cost of learning and the consequences of being wrong. Reversible decisions can generally tolerate more uncertainty than difficult-to-reverse commitments. The resulting need is treatment of the unknown through appropriate mechanisms such as research, evidence gathering, experimentation, prototyping, scenario analysis, and risk analysis. Different methods address different forms of uncertainty and should be selected according to the information actually required.

A decision changes more than the immediate state it targets. It can consume resources, establish commitments, introduce dependencies, create constraints, affect other parties, eliminate alternatives, preserve options, create new possibilities, and generate information for later decisions. These effects define changes in the space of possibilities: what remains feasible given current resources, commitments, dependencies, and constraints. Trade-offs arise because alternatives can improve one objective while worsening another. Optionality concerns the future choices preserved by a decision; reversibility concerns how easily its commitments can be undone. Time also changes available possibilities: delaying action may produce useful information but can consume resources, lose opportunities, or allow other constraints to emerge. Remaining in the current state therefore does not necessarily preserve the current set of alternatives, and choosing not to act can itself produce consequences. The resulting need is to understand and manage impacts through alternative and scenario analysis, trade-off analysis, dependency analysis, reversibility and optionality analysis, opportunity-cost analysis, communication, and coordination. The purpose is to understand how alternatives affect objectives, constraints, people, and future choices rather than to identify a universally superior option.

Decisions participate in an ongoing process of evolution because their consequences become part of the conditions for future decisions. Resources, commitments, dependencies, constraints, alternatives, and knowledge accumulate over time. Evolution does not necessarily mean improvement: a system can move toward its objectives while becoming more constrained, expensive, or difficult to change, and an organization can learn while simultaneously accumulating commitments that limit future options. Planning, execution, observation, and learning are therefore connected. Decisions may be maintained, revised, or replaced as evidence and circumstances change. PDCA and OODA illustrate cycles of action, observation, learning, and adaptation, although they are not Decision Architecture models. Repeated decisions create path dependencies, making previous choices part of the context for subsequent ones. In architecture, for example, a technology choice can later affect hiring, tooling, operating costs, integrations, and future architectural choices. The resulting need is observation and learning: determine what happened, compare outcomes with expectations, understand deviations, and preserve information that will be relevant to future decisions. Indicators, feedback, retrospectives, experiments, documentation, and decision records can support this. Traceability is useful only insofar as it preserves knowledge needed to understand and act on evolution.

The six foundations form an interconnected model rather than a sequence. Domain establishes the field of interpretation; context describes the conditions; objectives define intended outcomes; uncertainty identifies what is not known; consequences describe how alternatives change the current and future situation; and evolution describes how those changes become the context for subsequent decisions. The relationships are bidirectional: new knowledge can change the understanding of a domain or context; context changes can alter objectives; objectives determine which alternatives matter; evidence changes uncertainty; decisions alter future possibilities; and unexpected consequences can create new conditions requiring further decisions. The structure can therefore be represented as domain → context → objectives → uncertainty → consequences → evolution, provided this is understood as a conceptual relationship rather than a workflow.

These foundations provide a way to relate decision characteristics to practices and frameworks through the chain characteristic → need → practice → framework. Domain creates needs around boundaries and shared understanding; context creates needs around current conditions, constraints, assumptions, and dependencies; objectives create needs around evaluation criteria; uncertainty creates needs around learning, experimentation, and risk; consequences create needs around impacts, trade-offs, communication, reversibility, and future options; and evolution creates needs around observation, adaptation, and preservation of knowledge. Existing approaches can address several of these needs simultaneously. DDD, arc42, TOGAF, Scrum, Lean Startup, Design Thinking, risk management, retrospectives, and ADRs are examples of practices or frameworks that address different combinations rather than belonging exclusively to a single foundation.

The framework should therefore not be the starting point. First identify the decision, determine which characteristics require attention, derive the resulting needs, and then select practices or frameworks that address them. The foundations do not replace existing methods; they provide a common conceptual layer for understanding why those methods exist, what needs they address, and when their practices are applicable.
