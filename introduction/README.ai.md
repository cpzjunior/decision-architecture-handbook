The initial motivation for this work was to develop a methodological reference for Solution Architecture, similar to the role PMBOK plays in Project Management. Drawing on academic backgrounds in Project Management and Solution Architecture, as well as entrepreneurial experience in startups, I identified recurring challenges across these disciplines: understanding situations, recognizing constraints, defining objectives, evaluating alternatives, managing uncertainty, and assessing the consequences of decisions. Although each field employs distinct methods, these fundamental decision-making elements are largely shared.

This observation shifted the focus from developing discipline-specific methodologies to investigating the underlying concepts common to decision-making across different contexts. This led to the proposal of Decision Architecture, a conceptual foundation for understanding the elements involved in decisions and the relationships between them. Rather than replacing established frameworks such as PMBOK, TOGAF, arc42, or Lean Startup, the proposal addresses a layer that precedes their application, helping identify what should be considered when making decisions, independently of the domain or methodology involved.

The guide is structured around six foundations: domain, initial context, objectives, risks and uncertainties, direct and indirect consequences, and evolution. These dimensions provide complementary perspectives for analyzing decisions and should not be understood as mandatory sequential stages. Domain identifies the field in which a decision takes place; initial context encompasses existing conditions, resources, constraints, assumptions, and dependencies; objectives establish the intended outcomes and criteria for evaluating alternatives; risks and uncertainties address incomplete information and potential events affecting results; direct and indirect consequences examine impacts on people, resources, systems, and opportunities; and evolution considers how decisions transform existing conditions and influence subsequent choices.

The chapters explore these foundations individually, connecting them to practices and examples from Project Management, Solution Architecture, Entrepreneurship, and User Experience. The final chapter reverses the analytical approach by starting with concrete problems, professional activities, and personal situations, demonstrating how the six foundations can be identified and applied to different decision-making scenarios. This approach reflects how decisions typically emerge in practice: from problems, opportunities, or needs for change rather than from an already structured conceptual framework.

Decision Architecture is presented as an open and evolving proposal rather than a definitive methodology. Its concepts may be revised, expanded, and refined as new applications and experiences emerge. The terminology itself remains subject to change, as the current designation represents an attempt to describe the idea of structuring the elements involved in decisions rather than establishing a definitive theoretical classification.

The broader ambition is to encourage discussion and collaborative development beyond the scope of an individual initiative. Contributions from professionals and researchers across different disciplines could challenge existing assumptions, introduce new perspectives, and help refine the foundations, ultimately supporting the development of a shared body of knowledge around decision-making.
