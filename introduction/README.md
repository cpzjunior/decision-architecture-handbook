# Introduction

The initial idea behind this work was to develop a methodological reference for Solution Architecture that would play a role similar to that of the PMBOK in Project Management. I felt the need for an approach that would organize the discipline's knowledge and practices, providing solution architects with a reference to guide their work.

My education in Project Management and Solution Architecture, both at the MBA level, contributed to this perception. In the former, I encountered established methods and practices for organizing initiatives and managing resources, risks, and deliverables. In the latter, I found approaches for understanding business needs, evaluating technological alternatives, and structuring solutions. My entrepreneurial experience, particularly in the startup environment, added another perspective: the need to make decisions frequently with limited resources, incomplete information, and conditions subject to change.

As I moved between these fields, I began to realize that, despite their particularities, they dealt with similar fundamental issues. Managing a project, designing a solution, or developing a business requires understanding a situation, recognizing its conditions, establishing objectives, evaluating alternatives, dealing with uncertainties, and considering the consequences of choices. The methods employed are different, but many of the elements involved in decision-making are recurring.

This realization changed the direction of the work. Rather than developing a methodology specifically for Solution Architecture and, subsequently, others for Project Management and Entrepreneurship, I began investigating the concepts that precede these disciplines and help structure their practices. The objective was no longer to organize the work of a specific professional activity, but to understand the elements common to decisions made in different contexts. This shift gave rise to what I call, in this guide, Decision Architecture.

The proposal is not intended to replace existing frameworks or methodologies. References such as PMBOK, TOGAF, arc42, and Lean Startup address specific needs and have their own knowledge bases and practices. The focus of this work is on a layer that precedes their application: understanding which aspects need to be considered in a decision and how they relate to one another, regardless of the domain in which it takes place or the method used to carry it out.

This choice does not mean abandoning the possibility of developing dedicated works on the disciplines that motivated this investigation. Project Management, Solution Architecture, and Entrepreneurship continue to offer opportunities for further exploration. However, I considered it more important to begin with the broader perspective that makes it possible to understand their relationships, leaving discipline-specific studies as future possibilities.

## Guide Structure

The guide is organized into two complementary perspectives. In the first chapters, I present the foundations of the proposal and explore each of them individually. In the final chapter, I reverse the approach: I start with concrete problems and demonstrate how the foundations can be used to analyze them.

The first chapter presents the six foundations of Decision Architecture: domain, initial context, objectives, risks and uncertainties, direct and indirect consequences, and evolution. They represent dimensions that make it possible to understand different aspects of a decision and the relationships between them. They do not constitute mandatory stages in a process, but rather perspectives that can be considered together, according to the characteristics of the situation being analyzed.

The following chapters are dedicated to exploring each foundation in greater depth. The second addresses the domain, identifying the field of knowledge or activity in which the decision is situated. The third deals with the initial context, considering existing conditions, available resources, constraints, assumptions, and dependencies that influence the alternatives. The fourth explores objectives, their definition criteria, and their importance in guiding choices.

The fifth chapter examines risks and uncertainties, including incomplete information, assumptions, and events that may interfere with outcomes. The sixth broadens the analysis to encompass the direct and indirect consequences of the alternatives, considering their impacts on people, resources, systems, and opportunities. The seventh addresses evolution, examining how decisions modify existing conditions and influence subsequent choices.

Throughout these chapters, the foundations are connected to practices and situations from different fields, particularly Project Management, Solution Architecture, Entrepreneurship, and User Experience. The examples seek to demonstrate how the same concepts can be applied in distinct contexts while respecting the particularities of each discipline.

The eighth chapter adopts a different perspective. Rather than starting with the foundations, it starts with problems and areas of activity to demonstrate how they can be identified and used in the analysis of concrete situations. Scenarios related to the disciplines already presented are explored, along with situations from professional and personal life.

This reversal brings the proposal closer to the way we typically deal with decisions. In practice, we rarely begin with an organized conceptual framework. We generally start with a problem, an opportunity, or a need for change and must identify which aspects deserve attention. The chapter demonstrates how the foundations can help guide this process without requiring the analysis to begin with any specific dimension.

This guide is also part of an open and evolving project. The concepts presented represent the current stage of development of the proposal and may be reviewed, further explored, or expanded as new applications, experiences, and lessons are incorporated. I do not consider this publication a definitive version, but rather a starting point for organizing and sharing the ideas developed so far.

The term Decision Architecture itself also remains open to revision. I chose this expression because I consider it appropriate for representing the proposal at this stage, particularly because of the idea of structuring the elements involved in a decision. I recognize, however, that the term may evoke associations with other concepts and fields of study, leading to interpretations different from the one I intend to develop. I remain open to finding a more precise designation as the work matures. The current name is a way of identifying the proposal, not a commitment to a definitive terminological definition.

Finally, I would like the development of these ideas to extend beyond the boundaries of an individual initiative. If the concepts spark the interest of other professionals and researchers, I would be very pleased to see them discussed, challenged, and refined collaboratively. Building a community around the proposal, bringing together experiences and perspectives from different fields, would be a valuable contribution to its evolution.
