# 5. Managing Risks and Uncertainties

Imagine a trader facing a trading opportunity in the financial market. Prices fluctuate, new information emerges continuously, and conditions that seemed favorable just a few minutes ago may change. There is no way to know for certain whether an asset's price will rise or fall, but the trader must decide whether it is worth taking a position. To do so, the trader examines the available information, assesses the fundamentals of the analysis, considers different scenarios, and determines how much they are willing to risk. They also establish conditions for closing the trade if the market behaves differently from what was expected. Their goal is not to infallibly predict the next move, but to make informed decisions and keep risk within tolerable limits.

This approach distinguishes trading from a bet based solely on the expectation that something favorable will happen. A trader may make a profit on a poorly founded trade through sheer luck and incur a loss on another that was carefully analyzed. An isolated outcome is not sufficient to assess the quality of the decision. What distinguishes a rational approach from a bet is the process used to evaluate information, recognize uncertainties, and determine acceptable exposure. Without this process, the trade ceases to be a structured decision and becomes predominantly dependent on chance.

Decision-making presents a similar challenge. We do not have complete knowledge of reality, nor can we predict with absolute precision how circumstances will unfold. Even when we gather reliable information and conduct careful analyses, aspects remain unknown or may change over time. This does not mean that all choices are equivalent or that making decisions is an exercise in guesswork. It means recognizing the limits of knowledge and learning to act without confusing what we know with what we merely assume.

In previous chapters, we put on the biologist's hat to understand the scope of a decision, the investigative reporter's hat to uncover the context, and the navigator's hat to establish objectives. Now, we assume the role of the trader to examine the uncertainty that remains even after these steps. Understanding a situation, investigating its conditions, and defining a desired outcome do not eliminate the possibility of error. We still need to assess the reliability of the assumptions underlying our reasoning, recognize the risks involved, and decide how to deal with what we cannot control.

In solution architecture, a technical choice may depend on assumptions about access volume, integration stability, infrastructure capacity, or the evolution of business needs. In project management, deadlines and budgets depend on estimates and conditions that may not materialize. In entrepreneurship, an initiative may be based on the expectation that sufficient demand exists to sustain a new product. In all these cases, planning requires us to work with assumptions, even though we cannot guarantee that they are true. The problem is not accepting uncertainty, but ignoring it or treating expectations as established facts.

Managing risks and uncertainties therefore requires examining the foundations of our reasoning, assessing relevant exposures, and choosing responses proportionate to existing conditions. Some uncertainties can be reduced through investigation and experimentation; certain risks can be avoided or mitigated; others must be accepted because no viable means of eliminating them exists. The same uncertainty may also reveal opportunities worth exploring. Just as a trader does not control the market, a decision-maker does not have complete control over the reality in which a decision will be implemented. What they can control, within certain limits, is the quality of the process through which they assess possibilities and decide how to expose themselves to them.

## 5.1 Recognizing the Limits of Knowledge

A trader does not need to know every factor influencing the market to execute a trade, but they do need to understand the limits of their analysis. Prices reflect a combination of information, expectations, decisions, and events whose interactions cannot always be predicted. Even an analysis supported by historical data, indicators, and market knowledge does not guarantee that the next move will follow the observed pattern. Recognizing this limitation is part of the job, not a deficiency that can be eliminated through greater confidence or more calculations.

Every decision begins with a partial understanding of reality. Some limitations stem from a lack of available information; others arise from the difficulty of interpreting existing data or from the dynamic nature of the environment itself. A decision made today may encounter tomorrow conditions that differ from those underlying its formulation. Therefore, the quality of an analysis should not be measured by the confidence with which its conclusions are presented, but by the consistency of its foundations and awareness of what remains unknown.

This distinction is particularly important when past experience creates an excessive sense of confidence. A trader may have correctly identified a market behavior pattern on several occasions and conclude that it will continue to repeat itself. Historical performance provides useful evidence, but it does not guarantee that the same conditions will persist. Changes in liquidity, the economic environment, or participant behavior may alter the observed dynamics. Experience helps formulate expectations; it does not turn those expectations into certainties.

Recognizing the limits of knowledge also does not mean assigning equal weight to every possibility. Some hypotheses are supported by stronger evidence than others, and certain estimates may be more reliable when relevant data and relatively stable conditions are available. The important point is not to confuse probability with a guarantee. An event may be more likely than its alternatives and still not occur in a particular situation. Likewise, an unlikely outcome may occur without necessarily meaning that the analysis that considered it unlikely was flawed.

In practice, recognizing these limits means avoiding both unjustified confidence and paralysis in the face of the unknown. We do not need to know everything to make a decision, but we do need to understand which gaps may compromise the choice and which can be tolerated. The trader translates this awareness into exposure limits and exit conditions; in other domains, it may translate into additional validations, safety margins, execution alternatives, or criteria for reviewing a decision. In all cases, the goal is to act in proportion to the available knowledge without demanding a level of certainty that reality cannot provide.

## 5.2 Examining Assumptions

Every analysis depends on foundations that we do not always examine with equal care. Some statements are supported by evidence, others are inferences drawn from what we observe, and many are assumptions we accept in order to move forward. The problem is not working with assumptions, which is unavoidable when knowledge is incomplete, but forgetting that they are assumptions and building on them without considering what would happen if they were false.

A useful analogy comes from mathematics, whose formal systems are built on axioms: propositions accepted as starting points and used to derive other statements according to logical rules. Conclusions depend on the adopted foundations and the inference rules used. Changing the axioms may lead to a different formal system, with distinct properties and conclusions. The analogy with decision-making is not exact, because the assumptions used to interpret the world are statements about a reality that must be investigated. Nevertheless, it highlights an important point: reasoning can be internally consistent and produce conclusions that follow coherently from its assumptions without those assumptions corresponding to reality.

Consider a trader who expects an asset to appreciate because they have identified a pattern that, in their analysis, indicates an upward trend. The decision may depend on several assumptions: the observed pattern remains relevant under current conditions, the available information is sufficient to support the interpretation, and no significant change will occur that invalidates the analysis before the trade is closed. Even if the reasoning is consistent, these conditions may not materialize. The market may react unexpectedly, and the asset may lose value. The problem is not necessarily a logical error, but the possibility that the assumptions used do not adequately represent actual conditions.

The same situation arises in business decisions. A startup may project its growth based on the assumption that demand for its product will continue increasing at the observed rate. The financial projections may be mathematically correct, and the plan may have been prepared rigorously. However, if demand slows because the market is nearing saturation or a competitor offers a more attractive alternative, the expected results will no longer be supported by actual conditions. The calculations do not need to contain errors for the decision to be inappropriate; it is enough for a fundamental assumption not to hold.

For this reason, it is important to distinguish facts, hypotheses, and assumptions. Facts are statements supported by available evidence, although they may need to be revised when more reliable information emerges. Hypotheses are explanations or possibilities that we formulate and can examine through observations, tests, or other forms of investigation. Assumptions are conditions that we provisionally accept to develop a line of reasoning or plan an action, even when their validity has not yet been confirmed. In practice, these categories may overlap, but distinguishing them helps us understand the level of confidence we should assign to each statement and which ones need to be verified before proceeding.

Not all assumptions require the same validation effort. Some have little influence on the decision, while others underpin much of the reasoning. A trader may assume that the liquidity of a particular asset will be sufficient to close their position under the desired conditions. If this assumption fails in an illiquid market, exiting may be more difficult or may occur at an unfavorable price. In a company, a decision may depend on a supplier's continued availability or on the availability of a specific technology. Particular attention should be given to assumptions whose falsity would undermine the viability of the choice and for which reasonable means of obtaining additional evidence exist.

Examining assumptions also means recognizing that they can change over time. A condition considered reasonable at the beginning of a trade, project, or business initiative may cease to be valid following a market shift, regulatory change, or new evidence. Therefore, it is not enough to record assumptions during planning and treat them as permanent truths. We need to understand which conditions underpin the decision, monitor those that may change, and be willing to revise our reasoning when evidence conflicts with initial expectations.

## 5.3 Assessing and Managing Risks

In trading, identifying an opportunity is not enough to justify a trade. The trader must also assess how much they could lose if their analysis is wrong and whether that exposure is compatible with their resources and strategy. A favorable expectation does not eliminate the possibility of a loss, and a trade with high profit potential may be inappropriate when it requires taking disproportionate risk. The decision therefore depends not only on the expected outcome, but also on the conditions under which the risk will be assumed and the limits established to manage it.

Risk is associated with the possibility that an event or uncertain condition will affect the objectives of a decision. Identifying it requires considering what may happen, under what circumstances, and how significantly it could affect the intended outcome. The assessment may consider the probability of occurrence, the magnitude of the impact, the level of exposure, and the ability to detect or respond to the event. These factors help establish priorities, but they should not be treated as exact measures when the evidence does not support such precision. Assigning a numerical probability to an event does not automatically make the estimate reliable.

Consider the possibility of a meteor striking a company's headquarters and destroying it. The event is conceivable, and its consequences could be severe, but its probability for an ordinary corporate headquarters is extremely low. It would be possible to imagine specific protective measures against this scenario, but the costs would probably be disproportionate to the risk. The company does not need to ignore the possibility of a catastrophic event to recognize that devoting unlimited resources to preventing every imaginable threat would be incompatible with rational management. Risk assessment serves precisely to distinguish what deserves priority from what does not justify specific mitigation under the available conditions.

This does not mean that rare risks should always be disregarded. The decision depends on the combination of probability, severity, exposure, and recovery capacity, as well as applicable legal obligations and safety standards. In certain activities, even unlikely events require rigorous controls because their potential impact is unacceptable. In others, the most appropriate protection is not to try to prevent every specific event, but to reduce the organization's overall vulnerability. Maintaining backups in another region, for example, does not prevent the physical destruction of headquarters, but it can preserve essential information and facilitate the resumption of operations after different types of incident.

Responses to risks can take different forms. Avoiding a risk means abandoning or modifying the activity that produces it. Reducing a risk means lowering its probability, limiting exposure, or preparing mechanisms that lessen its severity. Sharing or transferring it may involve contracts, insurance, or agreements with other organizations, although these measures do not necessarily eliminate all possible liabilities and losses. Finally, accepting a risk means consciously deciding that the remaining exposure is tolerable in light of the expected benefits, available alternatives, and costs of other measures.

The trader applies this logic by determining position size, setting loss limits, and deciding under what conditions a trade will be closed. These mechanisms do not guarantee that the planned outcome will occur, nor do they eliminate all execution risks, such as abrupt price fluctuations or lack of liquidity. Their purpose is to establish discipline around exposure and reduce the possibility that a single decision will jeopardize more resources than considered acceptable. Risk management does not make a trade infallible; it makes the relationship between expected gains and the loss one is willing to bear more explicit.

Acceptance deserves particular attention because it can be confused with negligence. Ignoring a risk means failing to consider it adequately; accepting it means recognizing it, evaluating treatment options, and deciding that none offers sufficient benefit to justify its cost or restrictions. This decision can be rational even when the event would have severe consequences, provided that the analysis is proportionate, tolerance limits are clear, and no safety obligations preclude the choice. In organizational contexts, it is also important for acceptance to be authorized by someone with the authority to assume the exposure, rather than remaining implicit in decisions made without assessment.

Risk management does not end when a response is chosen. The conditions underlying the assessment may change, new information may alter estimates, and measures that were initially sufficient may no longer protect the operation. Therefore, the most relevant risks need to be monitored, along with the assumptions on which they depend. In trading, this means observing whether the conditions supporting the analysis remain in place; in projects and organizations, it may mean monitoring indicators, critical dependencies, and environmental changes. Review does not need to be continuous in every aspect, but it should occur when relevant signs indicate that exposure has changed.

The purpose of this process is not to produce an endless list of threats or create the expectation that every eventuality can be predicted. It is to focus attention on relevant risks, choose proportionate measures, and recognize the limits of possible protection. In some situations, the most prudent decision will be to invest in prevention; in others, it will be to prepare for recovery or consciously accept residual exposure. What matters is that the choice results from deliberate assessment rather than from the assumption that something we do not expect to happen will necessarily not happen.

## 5.4 Recognizing Opportunities in Uncertain Scenarios

Uncertainty is often associated with threats because what we do not know can undermine plans and expected outcomes. However, the same lack of knowledge that prevents us from predicting certain events also means that opportunities may exist without having been identified. In financial markets, a change in economic conditions, prices, or participants' expectations may create opportunities, but it may also invalidate previous interpretations. The existence of a favorable possibility is not sufficient to justify a trade; the underlying rationale and the exposure required to capitalize on it must be assessed.

Recognizing opportunities in uncertain scenarios requires a different approach from simply looking for risks. While risk analysis seeks to understand what may undermine objectives, opportunity exploration aims to identify favorable conditions that could generate value. In both cases, it is necessary to work with hypotheses, evaluate evidence, and recognize the limits of estimates. The difference lies in the purpose of the investigation: rather than merely protecting the expected outcome, we seek to discover alternatives that could enhance it or reveal paths not yet considered.

In entrepreneurship, for example, a company may identify an emerging need before sufficient evidence exists to estimate the size of the market. The absence of certainty does not mean the opportunity should be dismissed, but neither does it justify a large investment based solely on enthusiasm. A more careful approach may involve small-scale experiments, interviews with potential customers, prototypes, or tests of willingness to pay. These initiatives make it possible to turn some uncertainty into knowledge, limit the initial investment, and create conditions for deciding whether it is worth proceeding.

This logic also applies to projects and technology solutions. A flexible architecture may allow future capabilities to be incorporated even when they have not yet been fully specified. A team may test a new approach in a limited context before adopting it across the entire organization. A company may set aside resources to explore a possibility without committing the entire budget in advance. In situations like these, the decision does not have to be between acting with complete confidence and remaining inactive. It can create conditions for learning, preserve alternatives, and expand commitment as the evidence becomes more favorable.

However, exploring opportunities also requires criteria. Not every possibility deserves investment, just as not every risk needs to be eliminated. It is necessary to assess the opportunity's potential significance, the resources required to investigate it, the reversibility of initial decisions, and the value of the information that can be obtained. When the cost of an experiment is limited and the learning can guide future choices, testing a hypothesis may be more appropriate than trying to predict every outcome in advance. When an investment is irreversible or exposure is high, it may be necessary to gather stronger evidence before proceeding.

Managing uncertainty involves balancing protection and exploration. An excessively defensive approach may preserve existing resources but also prevent initiatives capable of generating value. An excessively optimistic approach may lead to disproportionate commitments to opportunities that have not yet been validated. The trader needs to consider both the possibility of loss and the expected return; in other domains, the same discipline helps compare the potential value of an initiative with the exposure required to realize it. In both cases, the decision should be based on explicit analysis without treating uncertain forecasts as guarantees.

## 5.5 Application in Project Management

In project management, uncertainties arise from the definition of scope through the estimation of timelines, costs, resources, and acceptance criteria. A schedule depends on assumptions about productivity, people's availability, requirements stability, and the execution of related activities. A budget depends on estimates of effort, prices, contracts, and supply conditions. Even when planning uses historical data and consistent methods, these conditions may change or prove different from what was expected.

Risk management makes these dependencies explicit and helps assess which ones deserve attention during execution. A critical activity may depend on a supplier for which no immediate alternative exists; a deliverable may assume that an integration will be available by a certain date; an effort estimate may rely on requirements that have not yet been fully detailed. By identifying these points, the team can seek additional information, create contingency buffers, prepare alternatives, or revise the plan before uncertainty compromises its ability to meet the project's objectives.

Not every risk can be eliminated through planning. Some conditions depend on external decisions, regulatory changes, or events beyond the team's control. In such cases, the response may involve appropriate reserves, contingency plans, monitoring relevant signals, or consciously accepting residual exposure. The choice depends on the nature of the risk and the project's ability to absorb deviations without compromising its objectives.

Assumptions also need to be monitored during execution. An approved plan does not turn its estimates into facts, and the occurrence of changes does not necessarily mean the original planning was inadequate. It means the initial decision was made using the information available at the time and that new evidence may require adjustments. Consistent project management does not merely compare actual results with the plan; it also examines whether the conditions that justified the plan remain valid.

## 5.6 Application in Solution Architecture

In solution architecture, many technical decisions depend on conditions that cannot yet be fully verified when the solution is designed. Choosing a technology may assume that it will continue to be supported, that it will be compatible with existing systems, or that it will meet anticipated performance requirements. A distributed architecture may depend on estimates of access volume, network latency, and the behavior of external services. An integration may be planned based on interface contracts that are still subject to change.

These assumptions need to be examined according to their relevance to the solution. When a choice depends on a little-known technology or a critical performance requirement, a proof of concept can provide evidence before a larger investment is made. When there is a dependency on an external service, it is advisable to assess its availability, operational limits, and alternatives in case it becomes unavailable. When future evolution is difficult to predict, decisions that preserve options for change can reduce exposure to premature commitments, provided that the additional flexibility does not introduce unnecessary complexity.

Risk management also requires distinguishing technical feasibility from operational suitability. A solution may work in a test environment and encounter difficulties when subjected to real-world volume, variability, and operating conditions. Therefore, load testing, monitoring, recovery strategies, and requirements validation are ways to obtain evidence and reduce certain uncertainties. Even so, it is impossible to guarantee that all future behaviors have been anticipated. The architecture needs to incorporate mechanisms to detect failures, limit their propagation, and restore operations when necessary.

In some cases, the most appropriate decision will be to accept a dependency or limitation because eliminating it would require disproportionate costs and complexity. This acceptance should be explicit and consistent with the organization's security, availability, and compliance requirements. The architect's role is not to choose a solution free of all risk, but to understand the relevant uncertainties, make dependencies visible, and guide choices whose limitations are known and manageable.

## 5.7 Application in User Experience

In user experience, uncertainties arise when interpreting needs, understanding people's behavior, and determining how they respond to an interface or service. Research and interviews can reveal expectations and difficulties, but participants' statements do not always correspond exactly to behavior observed in real-world situations. Similarly, a team may formulate hypotheses about whether a feature is understandable without knowing how different audiences will use it.

Treating these hypotheses as facts can lead to design decisions based on an incomplete view of the user. A team may believe that a particular step is unnecessary because it seems technically simple, while users struggle to understand its requirements. It may also assume that a new feature will increase engagement without evidence that it addresses a relevant need. Validation through usability testing, prototypes, and experiments makes it possible to examine these assumptions before committing resources to a broader implementation.

Not every uncertainty can be resolved before launch. Behavior may vary across segments, usage contexts, and conditions that were not reproduced during research. In such cases, the team can adopt a gradual rollout, monitor relevant indicators, and preserve the ability to adjust or reverse changes. This approach makes it possible to learn from real-world usage without assuming that results observed in a limited group will necessarily be replicated across the entire population.

Risk management in user experience also involves recognizing situations in which an error could undermine trust, exclude certain audiences, or make important tasks more difficult. The severity of these possibilities depends on the context and should not be assessed solely by expected frequency. A rare failure in a critical feature may require more attention than a frequent but easily reversible problem in a secondary feature. The analysis needs to consider the purpose of the interaction, users' needs, and the acceptable limits of the experience being offered.

## 5.8 Application in Entrepreneurship

Entrepreneurship involves decisions made under uncertainty because, in many cases, the business does not yet have enough historical data to support reliable projections. Market size, customers' willingness to pay, acquisition costs, retention, and the ability to scale operations may depend on hypotheses that can only be partially validated after the initiative reaches the market. A business plan can organize these hypotheses and produce coherent projections, but it does not turn estimates into guarantees of performance.

The analogy with mathematical axioms is particularly useful in this context. A financial projection may be structurally correct and still depend on an incorrect assumption about demand, competitor behavior, or customers' willingness to switch solutions. For this reason, it is important to distinguish calculated results from the conditions that must hold for those results to be plausible. The analysis should ask not only whether the numbers make sense, but also what evidence supports the assumptions used and how the initiative will be affected if they do not hold.

One way to deal with these uncertainties is to validate the most important hypotheses before making commitments that are difficult to reverse. Interviews, prototypes, pricing tests, and early versions of the product can provide evidence about whether the problem exists and whether the market is willing to adopt the solution. The goal is not to eliminate all doubt before starting, but to reduce the uncertainties that could invalidate the business model and limit exposure while learning remains insufficient.

Entrepreneurs also need to recognize that uncertainty can represent an opportunity. An underserved market may offer room for a new value proposition, although the absence of competitors is not, in itself, evidence of demand. An emerging technology may enable a different business model, but it may also introduce dependencies and costs that are not yet known. The decision requires balancing the potential to create value against the quality of the evidence, the cost of learning, and the ability to withstand unfavorable outcomes.

This logic favors progressive commitments: invest enough to test a hypothesis, evaluate the results, and expand the initiative when the evidence justifies additional resources. Rather than relying on a single forecast, the entrepreneur builds a learning process that allows assumptions to be revised and the business to adapt. The discipline lies not in avoiding every possibility of failure, but in understanding the main sources of uncertainty and not committing more resources than the situation warrants.

## 5.9 Application in Professional Career and Personal Life

Career and personal life decisions also depend on assumptions about conditions we do not fully control. Changing jobs may assume that a new organization will offer better development opportunities; pursuing further education may depend on the expectation that it will expand career prospects; moving to another city may involve assumptions about the cost of living, adaptation, and the quality of personal relationships. In all these cases, the decision combines available information, expectations, and aspects of the future that remain uncertain.

Examining these assumptions helps distinguish what has already been confirmed from what still needs to be investigated. Talking to people who know the organization, researching market conditions, assessing the available budget, or trying an activity before making a larger commitment can reduce certain uncertainties. These actions do not guarantee the desired outcome, but they make it possible to replace some assumptions with evidence and gain a better understanding of the conditions involved.

There are also risks that cannot be eliminated without giving up the initiative itself. Staying in a familiar situation may preserve stability, but it involves the risk of missing growth opportunities. Changing direction may open up new possibilities, but it may also require resources, adaptation, and tolerance for uncertain outcomes. There is no universally safe choice: even the decision not to act leaves a person exposed to conditions that will continue to evolve. The analysis needs to consider both the exposure associated with change and that associated with staying put.

In these situations, it may be useful to preserve alternatives and avoid irreversible commitments before the conditions are sufficiently understood. Building a financial reserve, developing transferable skills, testing an activity alongside existing commitments, or establishing criteria for reassessing a decision are ways to limit exposure without requiring absolute certainty. The suitability of these measures depends on personal circumstances, available resources, and the importance assigned to different objectives.

Managing uncertainty in personal life does not mean turning every choice into a formal risk analysis or attempting to calculate every possibility numerically. It means recognizing when a decision depends on fragile expectations, seeking information in proportion to its importance, and accepting that part of the outcome will remain beyond our control. Understanding this condition makes it possible to decide without demanding impossible guarantees and to change course when new evidence shows that the initial assumptions no longer support the chosen direction.
