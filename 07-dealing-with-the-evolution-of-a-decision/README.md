# 7. Dealing with the Evolution of a Decision

Imagine a chronicler who follows the development of a city of which they are a part. They witness the construction of new roads, participate in discussions about changes to neighborhood organization, and observe how the arrival of companies transforms the daily lives of residents. When recording these events, they seek to preserve not only what happened, but also the circumstances in which each change occurred, the motivations that influenced it, and the effects that began to emerge. Because they are embedded in the reality they follow, the chronicler also experiences these transformations and must update their understanding as new facts emerge. Their records preserve the city's trajectory and help explain how each stage contributed to shaping the current situation.

Dealing with the evolution of a decision requires a similar perspective. A choice changes the situation in which subsequent choices will be made. Resources may be consumed or created, commitments may be undertaken, dependencies may emerge, alternatives may be eliminated or opened up, and new information may be produced. These changes alter the conditions of subsequent decisions, requiring the context, relevant objectives, and available alternatives to be reassessed. The current state of a decision is therefore the result of a trajectory, not merely the initial choice.

This perspective follows directly from what we examined in the previous chapter. If decisions produce consequences that modify the space of possibilities, then the situation on which we make decisions does not remain static. Consequences accumulate, conditions change, and new information begins to influence how the problem is interpreted. What seemed appropriate at a given moment may need adjustment, while an alternative initially discarded may become viable. A decision does not need to have reached its final state for its evolution to be relevant. In practice, many decisions go through successive iterations, each carried out based on the conditions and knowledge available at that moment.

If, in the previous chapter, we assumed the role of the chess player to anticipate the consequences of a move, we now put on the chronicler's hat to follow and record how the situation changes with each iteration. The chess player examines how a choice may alter subsequent moves; the chronicler participates in the process, observes the changes, and preserves the elements needed to understand why the current state differs from the previous one. The two perspectives complement each other. Anticipating consequences helps assess what may happen as a result of a choice, while tracking evolution makes it possible to understand what actually happened and use that knowledge to guide subsequent decisions.

In solution architecture, this relationship becomes clear when a technology initially adopted begins to influence subsequent decisions. A team may choose a particular database to meet a product's needs at its initial stage. As the application grows, that choice begins to influence hiring, tool selection, operational procedures, infrastructure costs, and integration strategies. The current architecture begins to reflect not only present requirements, but also the decisions accumulated during its construction and evolution. Understanding this state requires knowing the trajectory that produced it and the reasons certain choices were retained, adapted, or replaced.

The same dynamic appears in project management and entrepreneurship. A project may need to revise its plan because an earlier deliverable revealed an unidentified dependency or because test results changed the understanding of the requirements. A company may modify its business model after discovering that customers use the product differently than expected. In both cases, the ability to adapt depends on recognizing what changed, understanding why it changed, and using the knowledge produced to guide the next steps. Evolution does not simply consist of continuing to execute a plan, but of observing results, recording lessons learned, and assessing whether decisions remain appropriate under existing conditions.

## 7.1. Understanding the Evolution of a Decision

The term evolution is used in this guide to describe a change of state over time as a consequence of decisions, actions taken, information produced, and changing conditions. This definition does not presuppose improvement. A situation may evolve toward the intended objectives, but it may also accumulate costs, constraints, dependencies, and problems that make subsequent changes more difficult. An organization may acquire important knowledge by executing a strategy while simultaneously undertaking commitments that reduce its future flexibility. Evolution describes the transformation that occurred, not a positive assessment of its outcome.

This distinction matters because changes do not always represent progress, just as maintaining a decision does not necessarily mean stability. An initiative may formally remain in execution while its assumptions cease to be valid, its costs increase, or its expected benefits become less relevant. Similarly, a decision may be revised without this representing an error in the original choice. If conditions have changed or new information has been produced, changing direction may be the most appropriate response to the current situation.

To understand this dynamic, we need to distinguish the initial decision from the state that emerges after its execution and the resulting consequences. The decision establishes a direction or authorizes a particular choice. The actions taken as a result change the situation, consume resources, produce results, and generate information. The resulting state then becomes the context for subsequent decisions. This sequence does not necessarily occur linearly: new information may emerge during execution, external changes may affect the results, and other decisions may alter the initially planned course.

For this reason, a decision should not be treated as an isolated event bounded by the moment an alternative is chosen. Depending on the domain, it may represent the beginning of a process of execution, monitoring, review, and adaptation. In other cases, the choice is a one-time event, but its effects continue to accumulate over a long period. In both cases, understanding evolution means observing the relationship between what was decided, what happened as a consequence, and the conditions that came to exist.

This perspective also makes it possible to distinguish three situations that may require different responses. The execution of a decision may need adjustments without changing its objectives or underlying rationale. The decision itself may need to be reconsidered because new evidence has weakened its assumptions or because its costs and benefits have changed. Finally, the context may change to the point where a new decision is required, with objectives or alternatives different from those originally considered. Recognizing these distinctions prevents every difficulty from being treated as an execution problem or every change in circumstances from triggering an unnecessary strategy review.

The current state, therefore, needs to be understood in relation to the trajectory that produced it and the conditions that exist now. History explains part of the situation, but it does not automatically determine the next choice. Previous decisions may contain assumptions that are no longer valid, commitments that no longer make sense, or constraints that can be removed. Knowing the past is necessary to understand the present, but it does not mean indefinitely reproducing the decisions that brought us here.

## 7.2. Observing Results and Learning from Experience

The evolution of a decision requires mechanisms to observe what happens after a choice is put into practice. Before execution, we work with expectations, hypotheses, and estimates. Afterward, we can compare those expectations with observed results, identify differences, and investigate their possible causes. This comparison produces information that may confirm part of the original reasoning, reveal limitations, or demonstrate that the situation was only partially understood.

Observation needs to be guided by the objectives and assumptions relevant to the decision. Measuring completed activities is not the same as verifying the results produced. A project may complete all planned deliverables without achieving the benefit that justified its investment. A product may record growth in the number of users without demonstrating that the problem it was created to solve is being addressed. A career change may provide higher compensation and still fail to meet expectations related to autonomy, learning, or quality of life. To learn from experience, it is necessary to observe what makes it possible to assess whether the decision is producing the intended effects.

Differences between expected and observed outcomes deserve attention because they may reveal problems of different kinds. A result below expectations may stem from inadequate execution, an incorrect assumption, a change in the environment, or an indirect effect that was not considered. Each possibility points to a different response. Improving execution does not necessarily resolve a problem caused by a flawed hypothesis, just as revising a strategy may be unnecessary when the difficulty stems from an isolated operational failure. The analysis must distinguish these causes before defining the intervention.

Planning, execution, observation, and learning cycles help organize this process. The PDCA cycle, for example, structures continuous improvement through planning, execution, verification of results, and action based on what has been learned. The OODA loop organizes adaptation through observation, orientation, decision, and action, emphasizing the importance of interpreting environmental changes before choosing how to respond. These are models with their own purposes and structures, not Decision Architecture models. Nevertheless, both illustrate how decisions and actions can form successive cycles of observation and adaptation.

These cycles also help explain why planning should not be confused with a definitive prediction of the future. Planning means establishing a direction and organizing actions based on the knowledge available at a given moment. Execution and observation produce information that may justify maintaining or revising that plan. The greater the uncertainty and the more dynamic the context, the more important it tends to be to incorporate learning into the decision-making process. This does not mean abandoning planning, but recognizing that its validity depends on the conditions that support it.

The frequency and depth of reviews should be proportional to the nature of the decision. A low-impact operational change can be monitored using simple indicators and frequent adjustments. A decision involving substantial investments, long-term commitments, or consequences that are difficult to reverse may require explicit monitoring criteria and formal reassessment points. In both cases, the purpose is to prevent execution from continuing merely through inertia when the evidence indicates that the course needs to be reconsidered.

## 7.3. Recording Iterations and Preserving Knowledge

The learning gained from the evolution of a decision can be lost when we fail to preserve enough information to understand what happened. A team may observe that a particular initiative did not produce the expected results, but without knowing the assumptions adopted, the alternatives considered, and the conditions that existed when the choice was made, it will struggle to explain the outcome. It may even repeat an approach that has already failed or discard an alternative that would be appropriate under different circumstances. The absence of records turns accumulated experience into fragmented knowledge that depends on the individual memories of those who participated in the events.

This is where the chronicler becomes important. Recording the evolution of a decision means preserving the elements that make it possible to understand each iteration and its relationship to previous ones. It is not enough to record that a choice was made or that a particular activity was completed. For significant decisions, it may be necessary to understand what problem motivated the choice, which objectives guided the analysis, which alternatives were considered, which assumptions supported the selected option, and which constraints influenced the process. Observed results, lessons learned, and the reasons for any revisions also help explain the trajectory.

The value of this record lies in the ability to recover the reasoning within its original context. A decision that appears inappropriate in light of current conditions may have been reasonable given the information available when it was made. The opposite also occurs: a choice that was initially defensible may continue to be maintained long after its assumptions have ceased to be valid. Without a record of the context and rationale, it becomes difficult to distinguish a decision that failed despite a sound process from one based on an insufficient understanding of the situation.

Because the chronicler participates in the events being recorded, the work also requires recognizing that the available knowledge changes throughout the process. An iteration may be carried out based on incomplete information and produce discoveries that change the interpretation of previous events. The record must preserve what was known at the time of the choice, without retrospectively replacing the original assumptions with explanations developed later. This distinction makes it possible to evaluate decisions according to the conditions under which they were made and understand how learning changed the direction taken.

Preserving iterations does not mean documenting every event. The volume of records alone does not guarantee useful knowledge. Extensive documentation can make it difficult to retrieve relevant information when there is no clear criterion for determining what deserves to be preserved. The goal is to record what may be needed to understand important decisions, explain changes, evaluate results, or guide future choices. The depth of the record should take into account the decision's significance, impact, reversibility, and the likelihood that other decisions will depend on it.

The mechanisms used may vary by domain. Indicators help monitor the evolution of results; feedback helps understand experiences and perceptions; retrospectives organize the analysis of what worked and what needs to change; decision records preserve the context and rationale of significant choices; technical documentation explains structures and dependencies; experiments produce evidence about specific hypotheses. These mechanisms serve different purposes and can complement one another. The choice depends on the type of knowledge that needs to be preserved and how it will be used in subsequent iterations.

Traceability emerges from the need to connect decisions, actions, results, and revisions over time. It makes it possible to recover not only the current state, but also the elements that explain how it was reached. In complex systems, for example, knowing an application's technical configuration may not be sufficient to understand why a particular architecture was adopted. In many cases, it is necessary to recover the requirements, constraints, alternatives, and decisions that influenced its construction. Traceability is valuable when it makes these relationships understandable and accessible to those who need to make new decisions.

This knowledge also needs to survive people's departures, team reorganizations, and structural changes. When the rationale behind important decisions exists only in the memories of the people involved, the organization becomes vulnerable to knowledge loss and the repetition of investigations that have already been conducted. Preserving the history reduces this dependence and allows new people to understand the reasons behind an existing configuration before modifying it. The record does not replace the experience of those who participated in the process, but it makes part of that experience available for future decisions.

## 7.4. Understanding the Influence of Previous Choices

As decisions accumulate, they begin to influence the conditions of subsequent choices. A technology introduced into a system may require specific expertise, favor certain tools, and make some integrations simpler than others. A long-term contract may reduce immediate costs but limit the freedom to change suppliers. An organizational structure may facilitate the coordination of certain activities while creating obstacles for initiatives that depend on collaboration across departments. In each case, previous choices help shape the set of possibilities available in the present.

This cumulative influence is related to path dependency. The central idea is that the path taken affects which options become viable, their costs, and the conditions required to adopt them. An initial choice may create advantages that reinforce its continuation, encourage complementary investments, and make change progressively more expensive. Over time, an alternative may continue to be used not only because of its current characteristics, but also because the organization has built processes, capabilities, and commitments around it.

Path dependency does not mean that the past completely determines the future or that previous choices cannot be reconsidered. It means that any reconsideration must account for the structures and commitments accumulated since the original decision. Replacing a technology may require data migration, integration adaptation, professional training, and operational changes. Changing a commercial model may affect contracts, distribution channels, and customer expectations. The alternative of making a change needs to be evaluated by considering not only its future advantages, but also the costs and consequences of transitioning from the current state.

This perspective is related to the optionality discussed in the previous chapter. Some decisions preserve the ability to change direction, while others establish commitments that make certain changes more difficult. Over time, this difference can become more significant. An apparently simple choice may create dependencies that restrict future alternatives, while a decision that requires greater initial effort may establish a more flexible foundation for evolution. The effect depends on the context, objectives, and uncertainties that need to be considered.

For this reason, evaluating a decision requires examining both the outcome it produces and the conditions it leaves for subsequent choices. An alternative may achieve its immediate objectives and still create constraints that need to be considered. Another may produce a smaller initial benefit but preserve important options or generate knowledge that reduces future uncertainty. There is no universal rule that determines which configuration is better. What matters is recognizing that the effects of a choice extend to the decisions it constrains and that these effects are also part of its evaluation.

The history of a decision helps distinguish commitments that remain justified from those maintained merely because they have already been made. The investment made in the past is relevant information for understanding the situation, but it is not, by itself, a sufficient reason to continue investing. The current decision needs to consider the future costs and benefits of the available alternatives, including transition costs and the consequences of remaining on the existing path. Understanding how we reached the current state should expand our ability to make decisions about it, not turn the past into an obligation to continue.

## 7.5. Application in Project Management

In project management, the evolution of a decision can be observed through the relationship between planning, execution, monitoring, and review. The initial plan organizes deliverables, deadlines, resources, and dependencies based on the available information. During execution, however, data emerges about actual productivity, task complexity, people's availability, and deliverable quality. This data may change the understanding of the project and require adjustments to the work sequence, resource allocation, or even the objectives themselves.

A team may, for example, decide to develop a set of features in a particular order because it believes that this sequence will reduce risks and facilitate product validation. During execution, a critical integration may reveal a technical limitation that affects several subsequent deliverables. The team must then reassess the plan, understand the consequences of the discovered dependency, and decide whether to bring the integration work forward, change the architecture, or revise the commitments made. The knowledge produced at this stage must also be preserved so that subsequent decisions take what has been learned into account.

In this context, indicators, change logs, retrospectives, and decision minutes help track the project's evolution. The goal is not to create documentary bureaucracy, but to maintain enough information to explain why the plan changed, which assumptions were revised, and what effects those changes produced. This record is especially important when team members change, scope changes occur, or decisions span different project phases.

Evaluating evolution also requires distinguishing deviations that call for correction from changes that justify a broader review. An isolated delay may be addressed through operational adjustments, while a persistent change in business conditions may require a review of the scope or expected benefits. Maintaining the original plan without considering accumulated evidence can turn execution discipline into resistance to adaptation. Conversely, continuous revision without clear criteria can undermine the stability needed to complete the work. The challenge is to use the information produced during execution to decide when to maintain the direction and when to change it.

## 7.6. Application in Solution Architecture

In solution architecture, evolution appears both in the transformation of systems and in the accumulation of decisions that shape their structure. A solution is rarely built all at once based on a complete understanding of every future requirement and constraint. It develops as new needs emerge, integrations are incorporated, technologies are updated, and limitations become apparent. The existing architecture therefore reflects a combination of deliberate decisions, responses to specific circumstances, and adaptations made over time.

Consider an application that starts as a relatively simple system, with few users and a small team. To reduce delivery time, the organization chooses a monolithic architecture and a technology widely known by the team. As the product grows, new requirements for availability, integration, and scale emerge. The initial choice may remain appropriate, but it may also impose limits that need to be evaluated. To understand the situation, it is not enough to compare the current architecture with technical standards considered desirable. It is necessary to understand the requirements that guided previous decisions, the problems they solved, and the dependencies that have formed since then.

Architectural decision records help preserve this knowledge. They can document the context, the alternatives considered, the determining factors, the expected consequences, and the conditions that would justify a review. When a decision is reconsidered, this history makes it possible to compare the current situation with the one that motivated the original choice. It also reduces the likelihood that a team will repeat discussions that have already been resolved or discard a solution without understanding the constraints that led to its adoption.

Architectural documentation needs to keep pace with the solution's evolution without attempting to reproduce every detail of its history. Diagrams, models, decision records, and information about dependencies serve complementary roles. The current model explains the existing structure; the record of iterations helps explain how and why it was built and modified. Both are necessary when the intention is to evaluate a change with an understanding of its effects and the commitments that will be affected.

This perspective is especially important when a solution undergoes significant transformations, such as migration to a new platform, replacement of a core component, or adoption of a new integration model. These initiatives do not start from a blank slate. They begin from a state built through previous decisions and must account for transition costs, available capabilities, existing contracts, and operational constraints. The ability to evolve an architecture depends as much on the technical quality of new choices as on understanding the path that brought the system to its current configuration.

## 7.7. Application in User Experience

In user experience, the evolution of a decision is related to how people interact with a product and the knowledge gained through these interactions. A team may adopt a particular interface solution based on research, behavioral hypotheses, and initial tests. After launch, actual usage may reveal difficulties that did not appear during conception, differences between user segments, or changes in expectations. This evidence may justify adjustments to the interface, the organization of workflows, or the very understanding of the problem the product seeks to solve.

Imagine a digital service that introduces a simplified registration process to reduce abandonment. The change may increase the number of people who complete registration, but it may also produce incomplete information that complicates subsequent stages of the service. Evaluating only the initial metric would lead to a partial interpretation of the result. It is necessary to observe how the change affects the entire user journey, what difficulties arise afterward, and whether the immediate benefit compromises other relevant objectives. The evolution of the decision depends on understanding these effects together.

Experiments, user research, behavioral analysis, and feedback help produce this knowledge. For it to guide subsequent iterations, it is also necessary to preserve the context in which each change was made, the hypothesis that motivated it, and the results observed. Without this record, a team may repeat similar experiments, interpret metric changes without considering previous changes, or lose track of why certain interface patterns were adopted.

The product's history also helps distinguish temporary preferences from persistent needs. A solution may have been adopted to address a technical limitation that no longer exists, or an interaction may remain in place because it was never reassessed after a change in the user profile. Understanding the trajectory does not mean retaining every previous decision. It means evaluating whether they still make sense in light of current evidence and recognizing which consequences need to be considered before changing them.

User experience evolves when teams turn observations into knowledge and use that knowledge to guide changes. The process requires more than collecting metrics: it requires relating results to the hypotheses, objectives, and decisions that produced the observed experience. In this way, each iteration can start from a more consistent understanding of what worked, what needs to be changed, and which conditions must be preserved.

## 7.8. Application in Entrepreneurship

In entrepreneurship, decisions are often made under conditions of uncertainty, with incomplete information and limited resources. The business model, value proposition, acquisition channels, and cost structure may need adjustments as the company learns from the market. A hypothesis about customer behavior may be confirmed, weakened, or replaced by another interpretation. Business evolution depends on the ability to turn this evidence into decisions without confusing persistence with insistence on assumptions that are no longer supported.

A startup may begin by offering a product to a specific segment and discover, during the first few months of operation, that another group shows greater interest or derives more value from the solution. This discovery may justify a change in positioning, but its effects go beyond commercial communication. It may change priority features, sales channels, pricing, required support, and the capabilities the team needs to develop. The decision to change the target audience modifies the state of the business and creates new conditions for subsequent decisions.

To understand this evolution, it is important to preserve the hypotheses tested, experiment results, market signals observed, and rationale behind changes in direction. This history makes it possible to distinguish an adaptation supported by evidence from a succession of changes motivated solely by passing impressions. It also helps identify which lessons remain valid even when a specific strategy is no longer used.

The record does not need to take the form of extensive reports. Depending on the stage of the business, it may consist of interview results, experiment metrics, records of hypotheses and decisions, or periodic analyses of indicator trends. The central criterion is to preserve information that makes it possible to understand what the company has learned and how that learning has changed its choices.

The trajectory of a venture also accumulates commitments. Contracts, investments, hiring decisions, brand positioning, and customer expectations may make some changes more costly than they would have been at the beginning. Recognizing these commitments does not mean avoiding them at all costs, but taking them into account when evaluating new alternatives. A strategy that is appropriate at one stage may cease to be the best option at another, and the decision to maintain or replace it needs to consider both the knowledge acquired and the effects accumulated during its execution.

## 7.9. Application in Professional Careers and Personal Life

In professional careers and personal life, the evolution of decisions can also be understood as a sequence of choices that transforms the conditions of subsequent choices. A person may decide to pursue a course of study, accept a job opportunity, move to another city, or devote more time to a personal project. Each decision consumes resources, establishes commitments, develops skills, and changes the available alternatives. Over time, accumulated experiences alter both objective conditions and the way the person understands their priorities.

A career change, for example, may begin with the expectation of gaining greater autonomy or better development opportunities. After a few months, the actual experience may reveal differences between expectations and working conditions. The person may discover that they value aspects previously considered secondary or that certain transition costs were underestimated. This information may justify adjustments to the plan, a change of role, or a review of professional objectives. The initial decision does not need to be retrospectively classified as right or wrong merely because the results differed from expectations. It is necessary to understand what was known at that moment and what the experience made it possible to discover.

Preserving the history of these choices can help identify patterns and understand changes in priorities. Personal records, periodic reviews of objectives, and reflections on significant experiences make it possible to compare expectations with results and identify lessons that might otherwise go unnoticed in daily life. The purpose is not to document every everyday decision, but to retain information that helps interpret significant choices and prevents accumulated experience from being reduced to imprecise recollections.

This perspective also helps explain the commitments that form throughout life. A course of study may open up opportunities while requiring time that is no longer available for other activities. A move to another city may expand professional possibilities and change proximity to family and friends. A financial decision may provide security over a particular time horizon while reducing flexibility to take on new projects. The effects do not need to be entirely positive or negative: they may combine gains, losses, and new possibilities that only become clear over time.

Reassessing a personal decision does not necessarily mean abandoning the chosen path. It may mean adjusting how it is carried out, recognizing that objectives have changed, or consciously accepting the costs of continuing. The history of choices provides elements for this assessment, but it should not turn previous commitments into permanent obligations. The current context, present priorities, and future possibilities need to be considered together with what has been learned along the way.
