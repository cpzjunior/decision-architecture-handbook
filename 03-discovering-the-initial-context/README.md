# 3. Discovering the Initial Context

Imagine a journalist who receives an assignment about a company that intends to expand its operations into a new region. The decision seems promising: there are signs of demand, the market shows growth potential, and the organization believes it is in a position to seize the opportunity. Before producing the story, however, the journalist needs to gain a better understanding of the situation. Where are the signs of demand coming from? Who are the potential customers? What characteristics make this region attractive? What conditions could facilitate the expansion, and which could make it more difficult? The opportunity that prompted the assignment is the starting point of the investigation, not a conclusion about the feasibility of the initiative.

Discovering the initial context of a decision requires a similar approach. A demand may arise from a problem to be solved, an opportunity to be explored, a change in the environment, or an idea that sparks interest. In all these cases, there is a situation that deserves to be understood before deciding how to act. The initial expression may be legitimate and relevant, but it may still represent only part of reality. Investigating means exploring that initial perception in greater depth, confronting it with other perspectives, and gathering information that makes it possible to better understand the conditions involved.

If, in the previous chapter, we put on the biologist's hat to observe an ecosystem, identify its elements, understand their relationships, and delimit the domain of a decision, it is now time to change hats and take on the role of an investigative reporter. The biologist seeks to understand the structure and behavior of the environment being studied; the reporter starts with an assignment and investigates the events, circumstances, and perspectives that help explain what is before them. One perspective does not replace the other. They serve different and complementary purposes: first, we seek to understand the domain to which the decision belongs; now, we want to discover what is happening within that domain and why a particular situation has come to demand our attention.

This distinction matters because decisions rarely begin with a complete understanding of what is at stake. In general, they begin with a demand, a perception of discomfort, an apparent opportunity, or a proposed change. Someone identifies something that deserves attention, interprets what they observe, and suggests a direction. In a company, the request may be to replace a system, automate a process, reorganize a team, or launch a product. In personal life, it may be to change jobs, begin a training program, or start a business. In all these cases, the demand expresses an initial perception of reality and provides a starting point for the investigation.

In solution architecture, this distinction becomes apparent when a business request is treated as though it already contains all the understanding needed to guide a solution. A manager may propose replacing a sales system because they believe the current technology limits productivity. The proposal may be correct, but it is still necessary to understand what is happening, how the difficulty manifests itself, and what outcomes the organization intends to achieve. The perceived slowness may be related to the application's response time, queries to external services, the availability of inventory information, a manual approval step, or the organization of the sales process itself. Investigation makes it possible to evaluate these possibilities without prematurely dismissing the proposed solution or assuming that it is the only appropriate answer.

Experience in project management reinforces the importance of this distinction. A project may begin with formally defined objectives, deadlines, and deliverables, but these elements do not guarantee that the need that motivated the work has been understood. Similarly, entrepreneurial experience shows that a business opportunity may seem promising while its core assumptions remain unvalidated. In both cases, the ability to execute well does not compensate for a misinterpretation of the situation. It is possible to deliver a project on time or launch a technically sound product without producing the outcome that justified the investment.

Discovering the initial context therefore means investigating before prescribing. This does not require knowing every detail of reality or indefinitely postponing a decision until all uncertainty disappears. It requires building an understanding proportionate to the importance of the choice, making explicit the available facts, the interpretations in circulation, the relevant conditions, and the gaps that still need to be considered. The goal is not to produce a definitive description of reality, but to establish a sufficiently reliable foundation for the decision to move forward with an awareness of what is known and what remains uncertain.

## 3.1. Investigating the Initial Situation

Every investigation begins with some manifestation of reality. It may be a deteriorating metric, a recurring complaint, a market change, a regulatory requirement, or the perception that a practice no longer produces the expected results. This manifestation draws attention to a situation, but it does not automatically explain what is happening. The first task is to understand what prompted the decision and how that motivation was formed.

It is useful to distinguish the observed event from the narrative developed to explain it. Consider a retail company whose managers claim that the sales system is harming store productivity. This statement may bring together legitimate experiences of salespeople, customer complaints, and unsatisfactory operational results. Even so, it contains several propositions that need to be examined: productivity has declined, the system contributed to the decline, and replacing the system is an appropriate response. Each proposition depends on different evidence. The existence of complaints does not, by itself, demonstrate the extent of the operational impact; a decline in productivity does not necessarily identify its cause; and identifying a cause does not automatically determine which intervention will produce the best outcome.

This separation makes it possible to investigate the situation without disregarding the person who raised it. The initial demand is valuable because it reveals what someone perceives as important, the outcome they hope to achieve, and the interpretation guiding their proposal. The problem arises when that interpretation is no longer treated as a perspective to be examined and instead begins to predetermine the boundaries of the analysis. When this happens, the investigation tends to seek justifications for the suggested solution rather than produce enough knowledge to compare different possible responses.

The starting point should be framed in a way that keeps the question open. Instead of assuming that the company needs to replace its sales system, we can investigate why completing sales is taking longer than expected and which conditions contribute to that outcome. This framing does not rule out replacement. It merely avoids turning a specific alternative into the definition of the problem. It also allows the investigation to uncover differences across stores, times of day, transaction types, or user profiles that might disappear in an excessively generic description.

The initial situation must also be understood in relation to what was expected to happen. A result can be interpreted as unsatisfactory only when there is some basis for comparison, whether a target, previous behavior, an operational need, a customer expectation, or an externally established condition. An operation may seem slow in absolute terms and still meet the business's needs; another may exhibit apparently acceptable response times but fail at critical moments when transaction volume increases. The meaning of the result depends on the purpose of the activity, the expectations involved, and the conditions under which it occurs.

This reference point is not always documented. In organizations, expectations may have been established informally, passed between teams, or embedded in practices that no one questions anymore. In a startup, the reference point may be a hypothesis about the behavior of a customer segment. In a career decision, it may be a personal expectation regarding compensation, autonomy, or quality of life. Making these reference points explicit is part of the investigation because it makes it possible to understand not only what is happening, but also why the situation has come to be considered relevant.

Investigating the initial situation should not involve seeking a single explanation before understanding the variety of conditions involved. The same outcome may result from independent factors, combined causes, or relationships that vary over time. Slowness in a sales operation, for example, may result from an unresponsive application under certain conditions and a manual approval process under others. Treating these manifestations as a single problem may lead to an overly broad intervention or one directed at the wrong factor.

The quality of this stage depends on the ability to keep plausible explanations open while gathering evidence to evaluate them. This does not mean treating all hypotheses as equally likely. It means avoiding giving the first available explanation an authority that the evidence does not yet support. From this standpoint, the next step is to organize the investigation around questions that allow the situation to be explored from different angles.

## 3.2. Formulating the Questions

Journalism has developed techniques for organizing investigations without assuming that the first account of an event is sufficient. One of the best-known is the 5W1H framework, which organizes questions around who, what, when, where, why, and how. Although it is used in different professional contexts, its contribution here is not to provide a mechanical checklist to be completed, but to help reveal dimensions of a situation that may remain hidden when the investigation is limited to the initial description of the problem.

The question of who involves more than identifying the requester or the person formally responsible for the decision. It is necessary to understand who noticed the situation, who is affected by it, who performs the activities involved, who possesses relevant information, and who has the authority to decide on or implement changes. These people may have different perspectives because they occupy different positions within the operation. A director may observe consolidated metrics; a store manager may notice variations between shifts; a salesperson may know specific difficulties encountered during customer service; a customer may experience consequences that do not appear in internal reports. None of these perspectives necessarily represents the complete situation.

The question of what seeks to define the event or condition that prompted the investigation. It is necessary to clarify what is happening, which outcome is considered unsatisfactory, what opportunity has been identified, and what change is intended. The formulation should be specific enough to allow verification, but not so narrow that it prematurely incorporates an explanation. “Sales are taking too long” is an initial description that still requires clarification: does it refer to the time needed to register an order, complete payment, locate a product, or finish the entire customer interaction? Each interpretation leads to a different investigation.

The temporal dimension, expressed by the question of when, helps reconstruct how the situation evolved. When was the behavior first noticed? Was there a recent change? Did the problem already exist on a smaller scale? Did it coincide with a process change, a technology deployment, a regulatory change, or an increase in demand? Chronology does not prove causality, but it makes it possible to identify events that warrant investigation. If delays began after the introduction of a new validation step, for example, that change becomes a relevant hypothesis, although it is still necessary to verify how it relates to the observed outcome.

The question of where seeks to locate the situation. A problem may occur in a specific store, in certain regions, in a digital channel, at a particular stage of the process, or in the interaction between systems. The location may also be logical, organizational, or technological. A difficulty perceived in the sales interface may originate in a service shared by several applications; a failure apparently confined to one team may result from a policy applied across the entire organization. Defining where the phenomenon occurs helps distinguish a localized condition from a broader characteristic of the domain.

The question of why requires particular care because it often invites people to present a cause before it has been investigated. At this stage, it is more productive to ask why the situation is considered a problem, which outcomes are being impaired, and what evidence supports that interpretation. The causal explanation should remain a question to be verified. When a manager claims that system slowness reduces sales, it is necessary to understand whether there is an observable relationship between service time and sales performance, whether other factors are involved, and whether the difficulty affects all transactions or only some of them.

Finally, the question of how seeks to understand the mechanism through which the situation manifests itself. How is the work performed? Which steps are followed? What information is produced and consumed? Where do waiting, rework, manual decisions, or external dependencies occur? How do people work around existing limitations? This dimension brings the investigation closer to the concrete functioning of reality and helps transform a general perception into something that can be observed, described, and analyzed.

The six questions complement one another, but they do not need to be answered in a rigid order. An interview may reveal a condition that requires observing the process; observation may contradict an initial explanation; a document may indicate that the problem began before the date recalled by participants. The investigation progresses through successive approximations, revising the questions as new information emerges.

In addition to these dimensions, three questions run throughout the entire investigation: How do we know this? What evidence could contradict this explanation? What do we still not know? They help prevent the 5W1H framework from becoming a form filled out with assumed answers. The technique's value lies in directing attention to different aspects of the situation, not in automatically validating the recorded answers. The questions constitute the investigation's agenda; interviews, observations, documents, and operational data are instruments for producing and verifying the answers.

## 3.3. Identifying Sources and Evidence

Once the questions have been formulated, it is necessary to identify where the information capable of clarifying them will come from. Not every person involved knows every dimension of the situation, just as not every document or metric was produced to answer the question being investigated. The choice of sources should consider the type of information sought, the source's proximity to the phenomenon, the limitations of its knowledge, and the possibility of verifying what it reports.

People are important sources because they know practices, exceptions, decisions, and experiences that frequently do not appear in formal records. However, individual accounts are influenced by the speaker's position, objectives, memory, and the categories they use to interpret events. A manager may describe the process as it was defined; an operator may explain how it is actually performed; a technical team may detail the behavior of the systems involved. Differences between these accounts should not automatically be treated as errors or bad faith. They may reveal that people observe different parts of the same reality, or that actual practice has diverged from the established procedure.

Documents and records provide another type of evidence. Contracts, policies, meeting minutes, reports, specifications, incident histories, service records, and operational data can help reconstruct decisions and events. Their existence, however, does not guarantee that they fully represent the situation. A procedure describes how work should occur, not necessarily how it occurs in practice. A report may consolidate information in a way that conceals important variations. A metric may have been defined to measure financial efficiency and be insufficient to explain the user experience. It is necessary to understand the purpose, period, coverage, and criteria used to produce each record.

Direct observation is particularly useful when there is a gap between the described process and the work actually performed. Following a sale in a store, for example, may reveal that the salesperson switches between applications, consults colleagues to confirm information, or repeats steps because certain data are unavailable when needed. These activities may seem like exceptions to someone familiar only with the formal procedure, yet they may constitute a recurring part of the operation. Observing the work also helps identify behaviors that participants themselves have stopped mentioning because they consider them normal.

Quantitative data complement these sources by making it possible to verify frequency, distribution, variation, and trends over time. Instead of relying solely on the perception that the system is slow, the team can examine response times, periods of peak demand, total service duration, failure rates, and differences between stores. However, the numbers must be interpreted in relation to what they actually measure. An application's response time is not equivalent to the time required to complete a sale, just as an average can conceal extreme situations that disproportionately affect certain users.

The strength of the investigation lies in combining independent sources, a practice known as triangulation. If salespeople report frequent waits, records show an increase in transaction completion times, and observation identifies a step where customer interactions come to a halt, there is a more consistent basis for understanding the phenomenon. Even so, this evidence does not automatically demonstrate that a single cause explains the problem. It corroborates aspects of the situation and guides the next stage of the investigation. If accounts and records diverge, the discrepancy is also informative: it may indicate differences in time periods, distinct definitions, measurement limitations, or experiences that aggregated data do not capture.

The quality of evidence therefore depends on the relationship between the claim one wishes to support and what the source actually allows one to conclude. An interview may demonstrate that a person experienced a particular difficulty, but it cannot establish the frequency of that event across the entire organization. A metric may reveal a trend, but it cannot explain its causes on its own. A document may confirm that a decision was formalized without demonstrating that its assumptions were correct. Recognizing these limitations does not diminish the value of the sources; it makes it possible to use them more precisely.

The investigation must also be proportionate. Not every decision requires extensive research, a statistical study, or interviews with every participant. The effort should take into account the impact of the decision, the cost of an incorrect interpretation, the availability of information, and the existing degree of uncertainty. A reversible, low-impact change may be undertaken with limited evidence and close monitoring. A decision involving substantial investment, regulatory risks, or changes that are difficult to reverse requires greater care in verifying assumptions. What matters is that the depth of the investigation be a conscious choice, rather than an accidental consequence of haste or information availability.

## 3.4. Separating Facts, Interpretations, and Hypotheses

As information is gathered, the knowledge produced needs to be organized. Without this separation, observed facts, opinions, explanations, and predictions tend to merge into a single narrative. The result may appear coherent while concealing reasoning gaps that compromise the decision. Distinguishing these categories does not mean eliminating interpretation, which is impossible in any analysis, but making explicit the role each statement plays.

For the purposes of the investigation, a fact is a statement supported by identifiable evidence within known limits. This definition requires care because a record may also contain errors, and an observation may be subject to particular conditions. Even so, it is possible to state, for example, that the available records show an increase in the average time required to complete sales during a given period, provided that the metric, source, and analyzed interval are clear. This statement differs from saying that the system became slower, because completion time may include activities that do not directly depend on the application.

An interpretation assigns meaning to facts. Faced with an increase in completion time, someone may conclude that the sales process has become less efficient or that the salespeople's experience has worsened. These interpretations may be reasonable, but they depend on additional criteria and assumptions. The increase may reflect a new verification requirement, a change in the transaction mix, or a change in how time is recorded. To evaluate the interpretation, it is necessary to examine these possibilities and establish which outcome is considered relevant.

A hypothesis is an explanation or proposition that still needs to be tested. The idea that an external integration causes some of the waiting, for example, may be formulated as a hypothesis if there are indications that make it plausible but insufficient evidence to confirm it. The hypothesis guides the next stage of the investigation: identifying the necessary records, observing the integration's behavior, comparing transactions, and verifying whether delays occur under the predicted conditions. A useful hypothesis is not merely an imaginable possibility; it must make it possible to identify what evidence would strengthen or weaken it.

This distinction prevents the authority of the person making a statement from being confused with the strength of the evidence supporting it. A hypothesis formulated by an experienced executive remains a hypothesis. A perception shared by many people deserves attention, but may still require verification of its scope and cause. Similarly, an interpretation presented by a technical team does not become a fact merely because it uses precise terminology. Specialized knowledge is essential for guiding the investigation, but it does not replace the need to relate conclusions to the available evidence.

It is also important to recognize that a lack of evidence is not equivalent to evidence of absence. If records show no failures, this may indicate that none occurred, but it may also reveal that the recording mechanism does not capture certain situations. If no customers complain about a step, one cannot immediately conclude that it causes no difficulty; perhaps customers abandon the process before submitting a complaint. The meaning of silence depends on the ability of the sources to observe and record the phenomenon being investigated.

In real-world decisions, the separation between facts, interpretations, and hypotheses does not produce a perfectly stable boundary. An interpretation can be tested and gain support; a hypothesis can be disproved; a fact initially accepted may need to be reconsidered when a measurement problem is discovered. The objective is to maintain traceability of the reasoning: to know what was observed, how it was interpreted, which hypotheses are being considered, and why a particular conclusion seems more plausible than the alternatives.

This practice also helps reduce the influence of biases. When an organization has already invested in a technology, those responsible may interpret evidence in ways that preserve the previous decision. When a department requests a new solution, it may prioritize information that reinforces the need for change. In a startup, the founders' conviction about a market need may lead them to select interviews that confirm the value proposition. Explicitly separating statements does not eliminate these biases, but it makes them easier to question and helps identify what would need to be true for the adopted interpretation to be correct.

A consistent investigation should seek both evidence supporting the initial explanation and information capable of contradicting it. This principle does not require indiscriminate skepticism, but a willingness to revise the narrative when the facts do not fit it. The result is a representation that depends less on the first impression and is more useful for understanding the concrete conditions surrounding the decision.

## 3.5. Understanding the Conditions of the Decision

Understanding the initial context means more than explaining what happened. It means identifying the conditions that influence the decision that needs to be made. These conditions may include objectives, constraints, available resources, deadlines, dependencies, obligations, interests, risks, and uncertainties. Some arise from relatively stable characteristics of the domain; others have emerged from recent events; still others result from the way the organization itself has structured the decision.

Objectives help clarify which outcome matters. A company may want to reduce service time, increase sales capacity, reduce errors, or improve customer satisfaction. These objectives may be related, but they are not equivalent and may conflict. An intervention that reduces the time required for each transaction may increase the risk of errors; a solution that improves the customer experience may require additional investment or operational changes. If the investigation focuses only on the most visible symptom, it risks overlooking the outcome that actually justifies the change.

Constraints define the boundaries within which alternatives must be evaluated. They may be financial, regulatory, contractual, technological, operational, or related to team capacity. It is important to distinguish actual constraints from preferences or historical decisions that have come to be treated as immutable. An approved budget may establish a real limit for the initiative, whereas a preference for a specific vendor may be negotiable. Integration with a legacy system may represent a necessary dependency, but transition alternatives may also exist. The investigation should recognize existing conditions without automatically turning them into definitive prohibitions.

Available resources influence the feasibility of possible responses. Time, budget, skills, operational capacity, data, and organizational support determine what can be done and at what pace. In projects, a technically appropriate alternative may be infeasible if it requires skills the organization does not possess and cannot acquire within the required timeframe. In a startup, a commercial hypothesis may be promising but require an acquisition channel whose cost makes the business model unviable. The analysis must consider not only what would be desirable under ideal conditions, but also what can be achieved within the available reality.

Dependencies reveal that a decision is rarely under the control of a single participant. A change to the sales system may depend on vendors, payment integrations, security policies, data availability, inventory processes, and the stores' ability to adopt new practices. These relationships may introduce deadlines, costs, and risks that do not appear in the initial description of the demand. Understanding dependencies also helps identify which information must be obtained from other departments and which conditions may limit the implementation of an alternative.

The interests and perspectives of those involved are also part of the context. One department may prioritize speed, another may value control, while a third is responsible for compliance or operational stability. These differences should not be reduced to personal conflicts. They often reflect distinct responsibilities, incentives, and success criteria. A decision that seems simple from one department's perspective may create costs or risks for another. Making these perspectives visible makes it possible to understand the tensions the decision will need to manage without assuming that all participants evaluate the situation using the same criteria.

Finally, uncertainty must be recognized as a condition inherent to the decision itself. Some information can be obtained through further investigation, such as the response time of an integration. Other uncertainties depend on future events, such as customer reactions to a process change or the evolution of demand. This distinction matters because not all uncertainty can be eliminated before acting. Some can be reduced through analysis; others require experiments, scenarios, monitoring, or reversible decisions that allow learning along the way.

The identified conditions do not need to be treated as an independent list of factors. They form relationships that alter the decision space. A regulatory deadline may limit the time available for investigation; a budget constraint may make an alternative infeasible; a lack of data may prevent comparisons of outcomes; a poorly defined objective may generate disagreements about what success means. Understanding the context means recognizing these relationships and their implications, preparing a foundation for representing the situation coherently.

## 3.6. Building a Representation of the Initial Context

The investigation produces different types of information: accounts, records, observations, metrics, interpretations, hypotheses, and conditions. For this information to support a decision, it must be organized into a representation that preserves what is relevant without indiscriminately reproducing everything collected. The initial context is not a transcript of interviews or a document repository. It is a well-supported synthesis of the situation that makes it possible to understand why the decision is being considered, which conditions influence it, and which questions remain open.

This representation should begin with the observed situation and the motivation for the decision. It is necessary to make explicit what drew attention, which outcome is considered unsatisfactory or desirable, and what change is being considered. The description should distinguish the perceived need from the proposed solution. If the original request is to replace the sales system, the representation may record this proposal as an alternative under evaluation while separately describing the observed difficulties in completing transactions. This approach preserves the possibility of investigating whether replacement is actually necessary.

Next, the representation should link the main evidence to the statements it supports. It is not necessary to include all the data collected, but it is important to identify the relevant sources, the periods considered, and the limitations affecting interpretation. If the analysis is based on service-time records, it should be clear what the metric measures and which steps are included. If a conclusion depends on interviews with salespeople from a few stores, that scope must be known. The representation becomes valuable when it allows another person to understand not only the conclusion, but also the reasoning that led to it.

The conditions influencing the decision should be presented in relation to one another, rather than merely as isolated items. A deadline may restrict the set of alternatives; a technological dependency may determine the implementation sequence; a difference between stores may indicate that a uniform solution will not produce the same results everywhere. The objective is to make visible the factors that alter the interpretation of the situation and the feasibility of possible responses.

The representation must also preserve unverified hypotheses and knowledge gaps. This prevents an unanswered question from disappearing in the final write-up and a provisional explanation from being interpreted as an established conclusion. If it is still unclear whether the delay results from the system, the process, or a combination of both, that uncertainty should remain explicit. It may guide further interviews, data analysis, operational observation, or a controlled experiment.

The appropriate level of detail depends on the decision. A low-impact change may require only a brief synthesis of the situation, the main evidence, and the known risks. A large-scale organizational transformation or architectural decision may require a broader representation, including different perspectives, dependencies, and scenarios. Rigor lies not in the length of the document, but in the alignment between what was investigated, what is being asserted, and the potential consequences of being wrong.

This representation should not be treated as a definitive artifact. The context may change during the investigation, and new evidence may alter the initial understanding. Data analysis may reveal that the difficulty is concentrated in a specific group of transactions; an interview may indicate that an operational change preceded the problem; a test may show that the most likely technical hypothesis does not explain the observed behavior. In such cases, revising the representation is part of the work, not a sign that the investigation has failed.

It is also important to preserve the distinction between the initial situation and the desired situation. The former describes the relevant conditions that exist when the decision is being considered. The latter expresses what is intended to be achieved. Confusing them may cause objectives to be presented as facts or current limitations to be treated as permanent characteristics. A clear representation keeps these aspects connected but distinct: it describes the starting point, makes the expected outcomes explicit, and allows the distance between the two to be evaluated later.

The initial context thus serves as a reference for reasoning. It provides a common basis for discussing the problem, comparing interpretations, evaluating alternatives, and recording why a particular decision was made. It does not eliminate disagreements or automatically resolve the identified questions. Its contribution is to make the discussion more precise by reducing dependence on implicit assumptions and allowing people to discuss the same situation, even if they continue to disagree about its causes or the best response.

## 3.7. Application in Project Management

In project management, understanding the initial context influences the very justification for starting a project. A project exists not merely because a deliverable has been requested or because resources are available to execute it. It is an intervention carried out to produce a change in a particular reality. Before organizing activities, estimating costs, and defining schedules, it is necessary to understand which situation motivated the initiative, which outcomes must be achieved, and which conditions may influence its execution.

This concern appears in different ways in management practices. Developing a business case seeks to establish the justification for an investment and compare expected benefits with costs, risks, and alternatives. The project charter formalizes authorization and provides an initial reference for the project's objectives and boundaries. Identifying stakeholders helps establish who influences or is affected by the initiative, while analyzing the organizational environment reveals conditions that may facilitate or hinder the work. These instruments serve different purposes, but they all depend on a reasonably reliable understanding of the situation to be transformed.

Investigating the context should not be confused with defining all deliverables in advance. In a system replacement initiative, for example, the initial scope may have been formulated based on the perception that the current application harms productivity. If this interpretation has not yet been verified, turning replacement into an irrevocable deliverable may compromise the project before detailed planning even begins. The investigation may reveal that part of the problem is related to manual procedures, insufficient training, complex business rules, or unstable integrations. The project decision may then change, whether through a scope adjustment, a combination of interventions, or the selection of a different alternative.

This does not mean that the project must remain undefined until all of reality is known. Initiation often occurs under uncertainty, and the organization may need to authorize resources to investigate the problem in greater depth. In such cases, it is important to distinguish the commitment to address a need from the commitment to implement a specific solution. A project may begin with the objective of reducing service time and include an initial diagnostic phase before defining the intervention. This approach makes explicit that part of the work involves producing the knowledge required for a subsequent decision.

Stakeholder identification also gains depth when conducted as an investigation rather than merely as the completion of a register. In a retail company, senior management may request the initiative, the technology department may be responsible for the solution, store managers may be accountable for operational results, and salespeople may directly experience the changes. Customers, payment providers, and support teams may also be affected. Listening only to sponsors and technical leads would produce an incomplete view of the situation, especially if the most significant difficulties emerge in the day-to-day work of the stores.

The chronology of events may be equally important. If problems began after a change in commercial policy, a partial deployment, or a shift in demand, these events must be considered before attributing the cause to the system. Incident records, operational metrics, interviews, and observation of the work can help reconstruct the sequence and verify whether the initial interpretation is consistent with what happened. The analysis does not need to prove a single cause immediately, but it should prevent the project proposal from becoming an explanation merely because it was presented first.

The context representation also contributes to risk management. Risks do not arise only during execution; some are present from the moment the initiative is formulated. A critical dependency may be outside the team's control, benefits may depend on behavioral changes, available data may be insufficient to measure outcomes, or the departments involved may disagree about what success means. Identifying these conditions from the outset makes it possible to establish proportionate responses, such as further investigation, technical validation, user testing, or scope reviews.

As the project progresses, the initial representation serves as a reference for evaluating changes and outcomes. If objectives are altered, it becomes possible to understand whether the change results from a legitimate reassessment of the context, new evidence, or a decision that has moved away from the original justification. If the expected benefits are not achieved, the organization can examine whether execution failed, whether the assumptions were incorrect, or whether external conditions changed. Without this reference, the project tends to be evaluated primarily on scope, schedule, and budget delivery, even when the initiative existed to produce a different outcome.

In project management, therefore, discovering the initial context is not a peripheral activity that precedes the work considered important. It is part of defining the work itself. The quality of planning depends on the quality of the understanding that supports it, and the ability to execute an initiative does not replace the need to verify whether it addresses the situation that actually needs to be transformed.

## 3.8. Application in Solution Architecture

In solution architecture, the initial context determines how the problem will be interpreted before decisions are made about components, integrations, platforms, or technologies. A solution is not appropriate merely because it meets formalized requirements or uses an architecture considered technically sound. It must address a real need, operate within existing conditions, and produce outcomes consistent with business objectives. To achieve this, the architect must investigate not only the system being changed, but also the events and conditions that led to the request.

Consider again the retail company that intends to replace its sales system. The request may have arisen because salespeople experience delays during customer interactions. An approach focused immediately on technology might begin by comparing platforms, evaluating architectural patterns, and estimating migration effort. These activities may be necessary later, but they do not yet answer the central question: which part of the operation is causing the delay, and what type of intervention could change the outcome? Without this investigation, the architecture risks reproducing the problem in a new technology or introducing complexity to solve a difficulty that lies outside the system.

The investigation must combine business, operational, and technological perspectives. Users can describe what happens during a transaction; process owners can explain rules and steps; operational records can reveal waiting times and failures; technical analysis can show how applications and their dependencies behave. Context diagrams, process models, system maps, incident records, and performance metrics can support this analysis, provided they are used to answer specific questions. A diagram shows known relationships, but does not by itself demonstrate how those relationships behave under real-world conditions.

Architecture practices such as those organized by arc42 help document a system's context and scope, its interfaces, constraints, and relevant decisions. Enterprise architecture approaches such as TOGAF provide ways to relate business objectives, the current state, the desired state, and the changes required. Domain-Driven Design contributes to investigating concepts, language, and domain boundaries. These frameworks can support an understanding of the situation, but they do not replace the investigation of the concrete circumstances. No architecture model, however comprehensive it may appear, automatically contains knowledge about the operational conditions under which the solution will be used.

The difference between expected behavior and observed behavior deserves particular attention. Documentation may indicate that an application queries inventory and routes payments to an external service. This describes an interaction structure, but does not clarify whether responses arrive within the required time, whether recurring failures occur, whether the information is reliable, or whether users must perform parallel activities to complete a sale. Contextual investigation complements the structural view of the domain with evidence about how it operates under specific circumstances.

It is also necessary to examine the conditions that limit architectural alternatives. A solution may need to coexist with legacy systems, comply with security and audit requirements, preserve vendor contracts, operate in stores with variable connectivity, or be deployed without interrupting commercial operations. Some conditions are mandatory constraints; others are organizational preferences that can be reconsidered. The architect must distinguish between them to avoid confusing the existing architecture with the only possible architecture. At the same time, actual constraints cannot be treated as secondary details because they influence costs, risks, timelines, and the feasibility of change.

The initial context also clarifies the relationship between cause and intervention. If the data show that the delay is concentrated in an external integration, a broad change to the sales application may not be the most efficient response. If the main difficulty lies in the repetition of manual steps, the solution may require changes to the process and operational responsibilities in addition to technological changes. If the system cannot support the expected volume, architectural evolution may be necessary. The investigation does not predetermine which alternative will be selected; it establishes a basis for evaluating which alternatives address the observed conditions.

There is also an important temporal dimension. The architecture must consider not only the current situation, but also changes already underway and those that may alter the relevance of the decision. A system replacement may be planned while the company is opening new stores, modifying its payment methods, or adopting a new operating model. These initiatives may create dependencies or make some alternatives more attractive than others. The initial context is not an isolated snapshot of the technological environment; it is a representation of the relevant conditions at the time the decision is being considered.

Once made, the architectural decision must preserve the relationship between context, reasoning, and consequences. Architecture Decision Records, known as ADRs, can document the situation that motivated the choice, the alternatives considered, the decisive factors, and the expected consequences. Their value depends on the quality of the investigation that precedes them. A record that presents only a decision and its technical justification may be insufficient if it does not explain which need the decision seeks to address and which assumptions support that need.

In solution architecture, discovering the initial context means avoiding a situation in which technology becomes the first and only language used to describe a problem. Technical analysis is indispensable, but it must be connected to how the organization operates, people's experiences, and the outcomes that justify the change. Architecture becomes more consistent when its decisions respond to an investigated reality rather than merely to a demand prematurely translated into requirements.

## 3.9. Application in User Experience

In user experience, the initial context is essential because the difficulties perceived by people do not always correspond to the apparent causes of the problem. A user may say that an interface is confusing, that a service is slow, or that a feature should be added. These statements are relevant sources of information, but they need to be understood in relation to the tasks the person is trying to perform, the conditions under which the product is used, and the steps that precede or follow the interaction.

A UX investigation therefore begins by understanding the experience being affected. If salespeople report difficulty completing a sale, it is necessary to observe how the activity takes place, which information they need to consult, which decisions they must make, and which interruptions occur during the interaction. The difficulty may be related to the interface's organization, but it may also result from inconsistent data, unclear business rules, approval processes, or limitations in integrated systems. The interface is where the difficulty manifests itself, not necessarily where it originates.

Interviews and surveys help clarify participants' expectations, needs, and interpretations. Task observation reveals behaviors that may not appear in accounts, especially when informal practices have become habitual. Usability testing makes it possible to verify how people interact with a solution under defined conditions, while usage data analysis may reveal patterns of abandonment, repetition, or difficulty. Each technique answers different questions, and the choice should be based on what the investigation needs to clarify.

Design Thinking and the Double Diamond, for example, provide structures for exploring problems, developing an understanding of needs, formulating definitions, and investigating alternatives. These approaches reinforce the importance of not converging on a solution before understanding the situation. However, conducting interviews or workshops does not, by itself, guarantee that the context has been understood. Research quality depends on participant selection, question formulation, observation conditions, and evidence interpretation. A study that asks only whether users would like a particular feature may confirm a stated preference without revealing whether the feature would solve a real difficulty.

The distinction between reported experience and behavior is particularly relevant. A person may say they want fewer steps in a form, but observation may show that certain steps prevent significant errors. Another person may claim to use a feature frequently, even though records indicate sporadic use. These differences do not necessarily mean that the person is mistaken. They may reveal that the person describes their experience based on memory, an exceptional situation, or an expectation about how they should behave. The investigation must understand these differences before translating them into design requirements.

It is also necessary to examine the service beyond the interface. A user journey may include digital and in-person steps, interactions with employees, waits for approvals, communication across different channels, and behind-the-scenes activities. A problem perceived during a purchase may depend on inventory, payment, logistics, or customer service. Journey maps and service blueprints help represent these relationships, provided they are not treated as complete descriptions without verification. The journey documented by the organization may represent the intended flow, while the actual experience includes deviations, exceptions, and strategies users have developed to complete their tasks.

Understanding the initial context also helps determine which users and situations need to be investigated. An experience may vary according to familiarity with the product, accessibility needs, physical environment, connection quality, or task urgency. Testing an interface only under ideal conditions may conceal difficulties that emerge in a busy store, on a less powerful device, or during an operational interruption. The relevance of these conditions depends on the decision, but they must be considered before generalizing results obtained from a limited sample.

When the investigation reveals that the difficulty differs from what was initially imagined, the problem definition needs to be revised. Instead of redesigning a screen to reduce the number of steps, it may be necessary to improve information availability or change the process sequence. Instead of adding a feature, it may be more important to make a rule understandable or ensure that an operation can be completed reliably. Research should not serve merely to validate a solution that has already been chosen, but to understand what people are trying to accomplish and which conditions facilitate or prevent that accomplishment.

The context must also be related to organizational objectives. Improving the experience does not necessarily mean optimizing a single interaction metric. Reducing form completion time may be undesirable if it increases errors; a feature appreciated by one group may create difficulties for another; a simplification may shift work to teams operating behind the scenes. The investigation should consider the relevant effects on the experience and the relationships between users, processes, and operations.

In user experience, discovering the initial context means understanding the situation in which the interaction occurs before concluding that the interface, user behavior, or absence of a feature explains the problem. This approach broadens the ability to formulate relevant problems and reduces the likelihood of optimizing one touchpoint while the difficulty persists across the experience as a whole.

## 3.10. Application in Entrepreneurship

In entrepreneurship, the initial context is often built around a perceived opportunity. A founder identifies a difficulty they consider important, notices a gap in the market, or imagines a better way to serve a particular audience. This perception may be the beginning of a business, but it does not, by itself, constitute evidence that a viable market exists. It is necessary to investigate who faces the difficulty, under what circumstances it occurs, which alternatives are already being used, and which conditions influence the willingness to adopt and pay for a solution.

A startup's initial narrative often brings several hypotheses together in a single statement. Saying that small retailers need an integrated platform, for example, assumes that this group faces a relevant problem, that current solutions do not adequately solve it, that a new platform would be preferable, and that customers would be willing to change their processes to use it. It also assumes that the business could reach these customers, deliver the value proposition, and operate with a sustainable cost structure. Each of these assumptions describes a different dimension of reality and needs to be examined appropriately.

Customer Development, Lean Startup, and Design Thinking practices offer instruments for investigating these questions, although they emphasize different aspects. Exploratory interviews help clarify problems and behaviors; observations may reveal how customers perform their activities; experiments allow specific hypotheses to be tested; prototypes and minimum viable products can produce evidence about usage, perceived value, and willingness to adopt. The build-measure-learn cycle is useful when what is built is linked to an explicit hypothesis and when the measurements make it possible to evaluate what was intended to be learned.

The investigation loses value when questions are formulated to obtain approval for the idea. Asking a potential customer whether they would like a system that saves time tends to produce favorable responses because the proposal is presented in desirable and abstract terms. Asking how they currently perform the activity, which difficulties they encounter, what happens when those difficulties arise, and which alternatives they have already tried tends to reveal more concrete information. Evidence about past behavior and actual decisions generally provides a more consistent basis than generic statements of future interest, although it too must be interpreted within its context.

The same care applies to market analysis. A broad segment may seem attractive because of the number of companies or the volume of spending, but these figures do not demonstrate that the audience is reachable, that its members share the same need, or that a specific solution can capture value. Small retailers, for example, may differ significantly according to their type of operation, region, sales volume, systems already in use, and capacity to invest in technology. The investigation must define the relevant segment and understand the conditions under which the opportunity may exist, rather than extrapolating conclusions obtained from a few participants to an entire market.

Existing alternatives deserve attention because the problem does not occur in a vacuum. Customers may use spreadsheets, manual procedures, competing products, outsourced services, or improvised combinations of tools. These alternatives may be inefficient in certain respects and still adequately meet customers' needs. Understanding why they are used, what costs they impose, and what makes them difficult to replace helps evaluate the value proposition realistically. A technically superior solution may fail if it requires a behavioral change that customers do not consider worthwhile.

The investigation must also examine the business model. Even if a genuine need exists and customers recognize the value of the proposition, the startup must reach its audience through viable channels, deliver the solution with quality, and sustain its costs. Customer acquisition cost, sales cycle, implementation requirements, support needs, retention, and willingness to pay are conditions that may alter the viability of the opportunity. A hypothesis validated in terms of customer interest does not automatically validate the other dimensions of the business.

Uncertainty is an inherent part of this process. The objective is not to eliminate every doubt before launching a product, but to identify which assumptions are most important and which need to be tested first. A hypothesis about the severity of the problem can be investigated through interviews and observation; a hypothesis about willingness to pay requires different evidence; a hypothesis about operational scalability may require delivery tests and cost analysis. The choice of experiment should reflect the question, not merely the ease of producing a demonstration or launching a feature.

A startup's initial context also changes as the business learns. Evidence obtained from early customers may reveal that the initially selected segment is heterogeneous, that the primary need differs from what was imagined, or that the solution requires a different distribution model. Revising these assumptions should not automatically be interpreted as failure or lack of vision. It is part of the process of building a business based on progressively more reliable knowledge of an external reality.

In entrepreneurship, discovering the initial context means treating the opportunity as something to investigate, rather than as a conclusion derived from the founder's conviction. Experience, intuition, and industry knowledge help formulate hypotheses and choose where to look for evidence. The viability of the business, however, depends on how these hypotheses withstand contact with customers, competitors, channels, costs, and concrete operating conditions.

## 3.11. Application in Professional Careers and Personal Life

Career and personal life decisions also frequently begin with a narrative about the situation. A person may conclude that they need to change jobs because they are dissatisfied, begin a training program because they believe they have lost their competitive edge, or start a business because they want more autonomy. These conclusions express legitimate needs and expectations, but they may bring different factors together under a single explanation. Investigating the initial context helps clarify what is causing the dissatisfaction, which outcomes are desired, and which conditions need to be considered before choosing a path.

A career decision, for example, may involve compensation, autonomy, professional relationships, type of work, growth prospects, stability, and quality of life. These factors do not carry the same weight for everyone and may conflict with one another. An opportunity offering higher compensation may require longer commutes; a position offering greater autonomy may provide less predictability; a career change may expand future possibilities while reducing income in the short term. Before comparing alternatives, it is necessary to understand which dimensions explain the current situation and which outcomes truly matter.

The investigation may begin by reconstructing the events that led to the decision. Did the dissatisfaction emerge after a leadership change, a change in responsibilities, a prolonged period of excessive workload, or the perception that growth opportunities had run out? Is the difficulty specific to the current job, or does it appear across different professional experiences? Is there a need that could be addressed through a role change, a negotiation, or training without requiring a complete transition? These questions do not presume that the person should stay where they are. They help distinguish the perceived problem from the possible responses.

Sources of information include personal experience, professional history, conversations with people familiar with the work, market data, job descriptions, and the experiences of professionals working in the areas under consideration. Each source has limitations. Personal perception is essential for understanding preferences and discomfort, but it may be influenced by recent events. Other people's accounts help reveal possibilities, but do not guarantee that the same experience will be repeated in another context. Market data may indicate general trends without representing the opportunities available to someone with a particular profile, location, and set of skills.

It is also necessary to distinguish facts from interpretations about one's own abilities. A person may state that they are not suited to a particular role because they experienced difficulties in one specific situation. This conclusion may reflect a skills gap, inadequate working conditions, a lack of support, or incompatibility between the role and their preferences. Treating the interpretation as fact may unnecessarily restrict the available alternatives. Similarly, believing that a training program will guarantee access to a new career is a hypothesis that must be compared against actual job requirements, market demand, and the experiences of people who have made similar transitions.

Practical conditions delimit the decision space. Financial reserves, family responsibilities, location, available time, access to training, and the possibility of trying a new activity influence what can be done and at what pace. These conditions should not be confused with an absolute determination of the future, but neither can they be ignored in favor of an idealized alternative. A transition may be feasible in stages, through side projects, gradual training, or changes in responsibilities before a definitive departure. The way the context is investigated helps reveal not only which options exist, but also which paths are compatible with the person's actual circumstances.

The same reasoning applies to decisions such as moving to another city, starting a business, or choosing a training program. The desire to live somewhere else may be related to career prospects, cost of living, proximity to important people, or the pursuit of a different routine. Evaluating only housing prices or job availability would be insufficient if other factors are decisive for the expected outcome. The investigation must consider the dimensions of reality directly related to the choice, without trying to turn every aspect of life into a measurable variable.

In this context, there is a particular risk of confusing desire with prediction. Wanting a change does not demonstrate that it will produce the expected effects; fearing an alternative does not demonstrate that it will be harmful. Desire helps define objectives, while evidence and analysis help clarify the conditions for achieving them. A well-founded decision does not need to eliminate the subjective dimension of the choice, but it should recognize when an expectation depends on assumptions that have not yet been examined.

It is also unnecessary to conduct an exhaustive investigation for every personal decision. The effort should be proportionate to the relevance, impact, and reversibility of the choice. A small decision can be made on the basis of limited information, especially when it is easy to change course. A change affecting income, relationships, location, or long-term commitments deserves more careful analysis. In some situations, a reversible experiment, such as undertaking a side project or speaking with professionals in a particular field, may produce more useful information than prolonging reflection without engaging with reality.

In careers and personal life, discovering the initial context means understanding the situation before assigning it a definitive cause or committing to a solution. This allows objectives and preferences to be considered without confusing them with evidence, constraints to be acknowledged without treating them as permanent, and alternatives to be evaluated based on the conditions under which they can actually be pursued.

Across all these areas, investigation serves the same fundamental purpose. It does not choose on behalf of the individual, replace professional judgment, or provide guarantees about the future. Its contribution is to make the decision more aware of the surrounding conditions, the evidence supporting the adopted interpretation, and the uncertainties that remain. Before deciding how to change a reality, it is necessary to understand how it presents itself, why change has become necessary, and which assumptions still need to be examined.
