# 6. Predicting Direct and Indirect Consequences

Imagine a chess player facing a complex position on the board. They identify a promising move, but they cannot evaluate it solely by its immediate effect. They need to consider how the opponent might respond, which alternatives will remain available, and how each response might alter the position of the pieces. A move that initially appears advantageous may expose an important piece, create a vulnerability, or lead to a position that will be difficult to escape. Likewise, a move that produces no immediate gain may prepare a combination of moves capable of opening up new possibilities a few steps ahead. The chess player must therefore analyze not only what their move produces, but also what it makes possible or likely in subsequent moves.

This way of thinking helps us understand an important dimension of decision-making. A choice produces more than an immediate result. It can commit resources, create dependencies, establish constraints, eliminate paths, or make certain changes more costly. Its effects can also propagate to other parts of the situation, affecting people, processes, resources, and decisions that initially appeared independent. Evaluating an alternative requires considering not only the outcome it promises to produce, but also how it changes the conditions under which subsequent events will unfold.

If, in the previous chapter, we assumed the role of a trader to understand how to manage risks and uncertainties, we now put on the chess player's hat to examine the ramifications of our choices. The trader needs to assess the exposure they are willing to accept in the face of uncertain outcomes; the chess player needs to anticipate how a move changes the board and constrains subsequent responses. The two perspectives complement each other. Even after recognizing uncertainties and assessing risks, we still need to understand how alternatives can change the landscape, produce cascading effects, and influence future possibilities.

In solution architecture, this need arises when a technical choice is evaluated solely on the basis of the immediate benefit it offers. Replacing a component may improve an application's performance, but it may also introduce a new dependency, require skills the team does not yet possess, or make future upgrades more difficult. In project management, accelerating a delivery may require reallocating people, changing the sequence of activities, or compromising other commitments. In entrepreneurship, lowering a product's price may increase sales, but it may also change margins, customer expectations, and competitors' responses. In all these cases, the decision must be examined in relation to the set of conditions it changes, rather than only the most visible outcome.

Predicting consequences, however, does not mean knowing in advance everything that will happen. Just as a chess player cannot analyze every possible sequence indefinitely, decision-makers must work with an analysis horizon proportional to the importance of the choice and the complexity of the situation. The purpose is to identify relevant effects, understand how they may relate to one another, and assess how each alternative changes the conditions for future action. This analysis makes it possible to recognize trade-offs, preserve important options, and avoid having an apparently advantageous choice compromise objectives that will become evident only later.

## 6.1 Understanding Direct and Indirect Consequences

A direct consequence follows relatively immediately from the decision made. If a company contracts a cloud computing service, it begins using infrastructure provided by a third party and assumes the costs and conditions associated with the contract. If a project reduces its scope, certain deliverables are no longer part of the planned work. If a person accepts a new professional position, they assume the responsibilities and commitments associated with the role. These effects are usually easier to identify because they are closely linked to the choice and often form part of the reasons behind the decision.

Indirect consequences arise through intermediate relationships. Contracting the cloud service may require changes to the architecture, security, and team skill sets. Reducing a project's scope may change the usefulness of the delivered product or require renegotiations with other departments. Changing professional positions may alter family routines, commutes, and future development opportunities. These effects are not necessarily unpredictable, but they depend on connections that may not be apparent when the decision is examined in isolation.

The distinction between direct and indirect effects is not absolute. An effect considered indirect in one analysis may be treated as direct in another, depending on the boundaries established for examining the situation. The important point is to recognize that the effects of a choice do not end with the first change it produces. An analysis restricted to the immediate outcome tends to overlook relevant relationships and may overestimate the benefits of an alternative or underestimate its requirements.

The chess player faces a similar problem. A move may directly attack an opponent's piece, but its value also depends on the responses it provokes, the pieces that become vulnerable, and the positions that become accessible. The immediate effect is only part of the analysis. To understand the move, it is necessary to examine how it changes the relationships between the pieces and how those relationships constrain subsequent moves.

In practice, this means that each alternative must be evaluated at more than one level. First, we identify what it changes directly. Then, we examine which changes may follow from those alterations, which stakeholders may react, and which conditions will be modified. We do not need to construct an endless chain of possibilities. We need to deepen the analysis until the main decision-relevant ramifications are sufficiently clear, taking into account the time, information, and resources available.

## 6.2 Analyzing Scenarios and Sequences of Effects

In chess, analyzing a move involves considering different responses from the opponent. A sequence that appears favorable if the opponent makes a particular move may become disadvantageous in response to another. For this reason, the chess player does not examine only the continuation they hope to find; they seek to identify plausible alternatives and assess how each one changes the position. The more important the move, the greater the need to verify whether its apparent advantage depends on a specific response from the opponent.

In decision-making, scenario planning serves a similar purpose. A scenario describes a possible evolution of a situation based on certain conditions and choices. It is not a guaranteed prediction, but a way to explore how reality might unfold and what effects might arise in each case. A company considering entry into a new market may examine how the initiative would perform under strong customer adoption, demand below expectations, or a more intense competitive response. Each scenario makes it possible to observe different aspects of the decision and identify conditions that might require a different response.

The usefulness of scenarios depends on the quality of the assumptions used. If all analyses assume, for example, that customers will adopt the product quickly, they will not adequately represent the uncertainty surrounding the initiative. It is necessary to consider plausible alternatives, including those that challenge initial expectations. This does not require imagining every conceivable event, but rather exploring relevant variations in the conditions underpinning the decision.

It is also important to examine sequences of effects, rather than only isolated end states. A change may trigger a response that, in turn, alters the conditions at a later stage. A price reduction may attract new customers, provoke a reaction from competitors, and require further reductions to maintain market share. The final outcome will depend on the interaction between these moves, not just on the initial effect on sales. Analyzing sequences helps identify situations in which an immediate advantage may lose its strength or create subsequent difficulties.

However, the ability to analyze scenarios is limited. The greater the number of variables, stakeholders, and possible responses, the greater the number of sequences that can be considered. Attempting to examine all of them may consume resources without proportionately improving the decision. The challenge is to select scenarios that help distinguish between alternatives, reveal significant vulnerabilities, or could change the choice. Like a chess player who focuses on the most relevant lines of play, decision-makers need to direct their analysis toward ramifications that can genuinely make a difference.

## 6.3 Recognizing Trade-offs and the Space of Possibilities

A decision can improve one aspect of a situation while making another more difficult. This relationship is known as a trade-off: a concession made in favor of one objective, feature, or possibility at the expense of another. Improving a solution's performance may increase its cost. Shortening a project's timeline may require more resources or increase exposure to failures. Increasing an architecture's flexibility may introduce additional complexity. In many cases, no alternative simultaneously maximizes all relevant objectives.

The chess player deals with this type of choice continuously. A move may gain space on the board but leave a piece unprotected. Another may preserve the king's safety but reduce offensive initiative. The value of each move depends on the position and the strategic objectives being considered. It is not enough to identify a move's benefit; it is necessary to assess what is being given up in exchange and whether that concession is acceptable in light of future possibilities.

The same reasoning applies to the space of possibilities associated with a decision. This space consists of the alternatives that remain available at a given moment, considering existing resources, commitments, dependencies, and constraints. A choice may reduce this space by eliminating paths or making certain changes more difficult. It may also expand it by creating capabilities, making resources available, or generating information that did not previously exist. Therefore, the consequences of a decision must be evaluated both in terms of what they produce and the possibilities they preserve, restrict, or create.

Consider a company that decides to adopt a proprietary technology platform to accelerate product development. The choice may provide ready-made tools, specialized support, and lower initial effort. In return, it may increase dependence on a vendor, restrict migration freedom, and raise future switching costs. An alternative based on open technologies might require more work initially but preserve greater flexibility. There is no universally better answer: the decision depends on the objectives, available resources, team skills, and importance assigned to future autonomy.

This analysis also helps explain optionality, that is, the ability to keep alternatives available for subsequent decisions. Some choices preserve greater freedom to adapt; others commit resources or establish dependencies that make future changes more difficult. Preserving options can be valuable when there is significant uncertainty about future needs, but this flexibility is not free. Maintaining multiple alternatives can increase costs, complexity, and coordination effort. The objective is not to maximize the number of options under all circumstances, but to understand which ones are worth preserving.

## 6.4 Considering Reversibility, Dependencies, and Time

Not all decisions have the same degree of reversibility. Some can be undone with little effort, while others require additional investments, renegotiations, or significant changes to reverse. A team can test a tool in a limited project and abandon it if the results are unsatisfactory. By contrast, adopting a platform that comes to support critical processes, store large volumes of data, and integrate multiple systems can make replacement far more costly.

Reversibility changes how a decision should be evaluated. When a choice can easily be revisited, it may be reasonable to proceed with incomplete information and learn from experience. When it requires commitments that are difficult to undo, it becomes more important to examine assumptions, compare alternatives, and understand the ramifications before proceeding. This does not mean that irreversible decisions should be avoided, but that they require analysis commensurate with the cost of a potential change.

Dependencies also influence future possibilities. A decision may tie an organization to a vendor, a technology, a contract, or a specific skill set. Over time, other decisions may be built on top of that choice, increasing the cost of replacement. This phenomenon is known as path dependency: previous conditions and choices influence which paths become accessible later. The current situation depends not only on what would theoretically be possible, but also on the path taken to reach it.

The chess player observes similar dynamics when a sequence of moves creates a position that favors some strategies and makes others more difficult. A poorly positioned piece may require several moves to reposition; an apparently minor exchange may permanently alter the balance of the game. Previous moves do not determine every future move, but they change the conditions under which those moves will be made.

Time, in turn, also changes the space of possibilities. Delaying a decision may allow new information to emerge and reduce uncertainty, but it may also consume resources, prolong a problem, or cause an opportunity to disappear. In other cases, acting too early may commit resources before conditions are sufficiently clear. The analysis must consider not only what happens if an alternative is chosen, but also what changes while the decision remains open.

Therefore, deliberately choosing not to decide can differ from simply failing to make a decision, but neither situation guarantees that the current state will be preserved. The context continues to evolve, other people may act, and certain alternatives may become more expensive or unfeasible. The decision to wait must also be examined in terms of what one expects to learn, how long it will take to obtain new information, and which opportunities may be lost during the wait.

## 6.5 Communicating Consequences and Coordinating Decisions

A decision that affects other people or groups cannot be treated merely as an individual choice. Its effects need to be understood by those who will have to implement it, adapt their work, or live with the changes it produces. Communication becomes an important part of managing consequences because it helps clarify what was decided, why a particular alternative was chosen, what changes are expected, and what responsibilities follow from it.

In project management, for example, a scope change may alter the work of different teams, require a new sequence of deliverables, and change previously established commitments. If these changes are communicated incompletely, each department may continue operating under different assumptions, generating priority conflicts and rework. The decision may be technically sound, but its implementation will be compromised if the affected people do not understand how it changes their responsibilities and dependencies.

Communication also makes it possible to reveal consequences that were not initially considered. An implementation team may identify operational constraints that were not apparent during the analysis. A user may point out difficulties resulting from an interface change. A business partner may clarify that a particular contractual condition affects the feasibility of an alternative. Listening to these perspectives broadens our understanding of the situation and may lead to revising the choice before commitments become difficult to reverse.

This does not mean that every decision must be negotiated with everyone involved or that communication eliminates disagreements. People and groups may have different objectives, interpret the same facts differently, and assign different values to trade-offs. The purpose is to make relevant effects understandable, explain the reasons behind the choice, and coordinate the actions needed to implement it. When legitimate conflicts exist, they must be acknowledged and addressed according to the responsibilities and criteria guiding the decision.

Communication must also keep pace with how the situation evolves. If conditions change, assumptions are no longer confirmed, or observed effects differ from expectations, the affected people need to understand what has changed and why the decision is being reconsidered. In this way, communication is not limited to announcing a decision that has already been finalized. It helps align expectations, coordinate responses, and maintain a shared understanding of the conditions guiding subsequent actions.

## 6.6 Application in Project Management

In project management, a decision rarely affects only the activity in which it was made. Changing the scope can modify the schedule, resource allocation, acceptance criteria, and coordination between teams. Shortening a delivery timeline may require parallelizing activities that would otherwise be sequential, increase the need for validation, or reassign professionals from other workstreams. An apparently localized change can therefore produce effects across the project as a whole.

Predicting these consequences requires examining dependencies between activities and understanding how a change propagates through the plan. Before approving a change, the project manager needs to assess not only the expected benefit, but also which deliverables will be affected, which commitments will need to be renegotiated, and which constraints may arise. The schedule is not merely a list of dates; it represents relationships between activities, resources, and the conditions necessary for work to progress.

Trade-offs become especially visible when schedule, cost, scope, and quality come into conflict. Accelerating a delivery may require more resources, reducing scope, or increasing exposure to problems. Preserving all deliverables within the original timeline may be unfeasible if additional requirements or technical difficulties arise. The purpose of the analysis is not to assume that all objectives can be maintained, but to make the necessary concessions explicit and assess which alternatives best preserve the highest-priority outcomes.

Reversibility also matters. A decision made early in a project, when few dependencies exist, may be relatively easy to adjust. The same change at an advanced stage may require rework, contractual renegotiation, and team reorganization. For this reason, decisions with significant potential to propagate deserve special attention before other activities are built on top of them.

Finally, the consequences must be communicated to stakeholders. Planning changes require teams, sponsors, suppliers, and customers to understand what has changed and how it affects their responsibilities. Consistent management combines alternative analysis, dependency assessment, and clear communication to prevent a local decision from creating misalignment elsewhere in the project.

## 6.7 Application in Solution Architecture

In solution architecture, technical decisions create relationships that can influence system evolution for many years. Choosing a technology, defining service boundaries, establishing a data model, or adopting a vendor affects more than the immediate implementation. These choices can determine how new features will be incorporated, how failures will be isolated, which skills will be required, and how much effort will be needed to replace components in the future.

An architecture based on independent services, for example, may allow parts of the system to evolve separately. However, this independence depends on well-defined contracts, observability, communication mechanisms, and the operational capacity to manage distributed components. The decision may increase flexibility in certain respects while introducing coordination costs and operational complexity. Evaluating only the benefit of separation, without examining its requirements, produces an incomplete view of the alternative.

The same reasoning applies to the choice between building and buying a solution. Purchasing an off-the-shelf product may reduce implementation time but create vendor dependency and limit the ability to customize features. Developing internally may provide greater control but require investment, maintenance, and skills that the organization will need to retain. The analysis must consider both current requirements and changes that may be necessary, without assuming that maximum flexibility is always desirable.

Architectural decisions can also create path dependencies. When multiple systems begin to depend on an interface, a data model, or a central service, replacing it may require coordinated changes throughout the solution. This coupling may be acceptable when it simplifies operations and meets business needs, but it must be recognized as part of the consequences of the choice. The issue is not to avoid all dependencies, which is impractical, but to understand which dependencies are deliberate, which constrain evolution, and how much it will cost to change them.

Anticipating these consequences does not require predicting every future requirement. It requires identifying decisions that may significantly limit the solution's evolution, evaluating alternatives, and preserving flexibility where it has concrete value. A proof of concept, a modular architecture, or gradual adoption of a technology can help generate learning before expanding the commitment. The most appropriate architectural decision is one whose benefits, costs, and future constraints are compatible with the organization's objectives and conditions.

## 6.8 Application in User Experience

In user experience, a change to the interface can alter more than the interaction directly affected. A new step in a flow may increase the amount of information collected, but it may also increase the effort required from the user. A simplification may reduce the number of actions needed, but remove guidance that is important for people unfamiliar with the system. An automated recommendation may speed up a task, but reduce transparency regarding how a particular result was produced.

Predicting these consequences requires examining how the change relates to user needs, other parts of the experience, and product objectives. A feature that improves an isolated metric may harm the experience elsewhere. Increasing the number of clicks on a recommendation, for example, does not necessarily demonstrate that it is useful; it may reflect curiosity, difficulty understanding it, or a lack of clear alternatives. Results must be interpreted in light of observed behavior and the conditions under which it occurs.

Indirect consequences may also emerge over time. An interface that encourages users to complete a task quickly may reduce errors in a particular context but make it harder to review important information. A change to the navigation system may improve the experience for frequent users while making the product more difficult to use for occasional users. Testing with different user profiles and contexts helps identify these effects before rolling out the change more broadly.

Reversibility is useful in this domain. Instead of immediately replacing an experience used by the entire customer base, the team can test the new approach with a limited group, monitor metrics, and gather qualitative evidence. This gradual implementation preserves the ability to adjust or reverse the change if the results reveal previously unanticipated problems. However, experimentation must respect privacy, accessibility, and applicable ethical constraints, especially when decisions affect vulnerable users or high-stakes tasks.

Communication is also part of the experience. Changes to features, rules, or flows need to be understandable to the people affected. When a change alters expected behavior, the absence of an explanation can cause confusion and reduce trust in the product. Predicting consequences in user experience therefore means considering not only how the interface works, but also how people will understand, use, and incorporate the change into their activities.

## 6.9 Application in Entrepreneurship

In entrepreneurship, decisions about pricing, positioning, sales channels, hiring, and expansion can alter a business's trajectory. A price reduction may increase demand but also decrease the contribution margin and change the product's perceived value. Entering a new market may expand the potential customer base but require regulatory adaptations, additional support, and a different sales structure. Hiring someone may increase execution capacity while also raising fixed costs and reducing financial flexibility.

Evaluating these choices requires examining how immediate effects may trigger new needs or responses. A company that rapidly expands its customer base may need to invest in customer service, infrastructure, and processes that were unnecessary at a smaller scale. If growth is not accompanied by sufficient resources and operational capacity, expansion may put pressure on service quality and compromise retention. Increased demand is a favorable outcome, but its ramifications must be considered to determine whether the business can sustain it.

The responses of other stakeholders also matter. Competitors may react to a new pricing strategy, suppliers may change commercial terms, and customers may adjust their expectations based on the adopted positioning. Entrepreneurs do not make decisions in a static environment; their choices change the behavior of other participants, who in turn alter the available conditions. Scenario analysis helps explore these interactions without assuming that other stakeholders will remain passive.

Optionality can be particularly valuable for businesses that are still taking shape. Testing a market segment before expanding operations, launching a limited version of the product, or establishing partnerships that do not impose excessive commitments can preserve alternatives while the company learns. On the other hand, keeping too many possibilities open indefinitely can scatter resources and prevent the business from developing a consistent value proposition. The challenge is to preserve options that could change future decisions without compromising the ability to execute the chosen strategy.

Predicting consequences in entrepreneurship does not mean building a plan capable of anticipating every development in the market. It means understanding the main relationships between decisions, identifying commitments that may constrain the business, and assessing how different scenarios would change the strategy. This analysis helps distinguish opportunities that can be pursued progressively from those that require substantial commitments before sufficient evidence is available.

## 6.10 Application in Professional Careers and Personal Life

Personal decisions also produce effects that extend beyond the immediate objective. Accepting a job may increase income and expand development opportunities, but change daily routines, the time available for family, and where one lives. Starting a training program may open professional paths but require financial resources and reduce the time devoted to other activities. Moving to another city may offer new opportunities but change support networks, living costs, and personal relationships.

These consequences do not need to be treated as reasons to avoid change. They help clarify what each alternative requires and how it relates to a person's other objectives. A choice that favors professional growth may conflict with financial stability or availability for family commitments. The value of the alternative depends on the priorities involved and the concessions the person is willing to make, not just on the most obvious benefit.

Time and reversibility also influence these decisions. A change that can be tried temporarily offers different conditions from one that requires selling a property, giving up a source of income, or assuming long-term financial commitments. When possible, testing an alternative on a limited scale can reveal important information before a more difficult-to-reverse change. This may involve trying a side activity, talking to people who already live the desired reality, or concretely assessing the costs of a transition.

It is also necessary to consider how the decision affects other people. A career change may alter shared responsibilities, require reorganizing family routines, or modify commitments made to others. Communicating the reasons for the choice, clarifying what may change, and listening to the concerns of those involved does not eliminate conflicts, but it allows the consequences to be better understood and the necessary adjustments to be coordinated.

Finally, the analysis must include the cost of remaining in the current situation. Delaying a change may preserve short-term stability, but it may also limit skills development or cause an opportunity to disappear. Inaction does not freeze the context or keep the same alternatives available indefinitely. Considering the ramifications of acting and waiting helps build a more complete view of the choices, without assuming that there is a path free of concessions or uncertainties.
